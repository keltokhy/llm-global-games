    Speaking in Code: How Surveillance Suppresses Coordinated Dissent
                                                 Khaled Eltokhy
                                            Department of Economics
                                           The Graduate Center, CUNY
                                                       May 2026


Abstract
 Surveillance and censorship are documented features of authoritarian information control (King et al., 2013; Penney,
2016; Stoycheff, 2016), but field data confound two channels: surveillance can change the private payoff of participation,
 or it can change what people say to one another before they decide. I isolate the second channel in a controlled
 LLM-agent analogue of the Morris–Shin (2003) regime-change game. First, agents reproduce the canonical comparative
 static: participation falls as regime strength rises (mean r(J, A(θ)) = +0.80 across 7 models). Second, adding pre-play
 communication makes private information social and raises joining in matched task cells (∆ = +2.10 pp). Third,
 adding surveillance only to the communication prompt reduces participation in 5/5 models (mean ∆ = −8.6 pp),
while the receiver-side decision prompt is unchanged and stateless. In the primary belief sample, reported expectations
 about how many others will join are approximately stable (31.2% vs. 30.9%, p = 0.5866), while actions fall by 12.9 pp.
 Message content shifts from direct (“regime,” “security forces,” “streets”) to coded (“walls cracking,” “ground shifting,”
“heads down”) language; explicit weakness references rise in the primary model. The result is a belief–action wedge in a
 language-mediated coordination game: surveillance leaves information present but makes it less usable for coordinated
 action.

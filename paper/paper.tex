\begin{abstract}
Authoritarian regimes can suppress coordinated political action either by directly deterring would-be dissenters or by poisoning the channels through which they communicate. The two mechanisms look similar in field data, where institutional and selection differences confound everything. I isolate the second using LLM agents in the Morris--Shin (2003) regime-change game: in clean prompt-isolation reruns, appending a monitoring warning to the \emph{communication} prompt---leaving the \emph{decision} prompt unchanged---lowers joining in \PromptIsoNegativeModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ models (mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp). Placebo (research-only) and anonymous monitoring warnings do not replicate the effect. In the primary model, surveillance lies \emph{below} a no-message control by \PromptIsoVsNoMsgDeltaAbsPP~pp ($p < 0.01$): surveilled messages are strictly worse for coordination than no peer messages at all---active channel poisoning, not mere suppression of information flow. A logistic classifier distinguishes baseline from surveillance messages at \MsgClassifierAcc\% accuracy on $N = \MsgClassifierNTest$ held-out messages (primary model): the shift is from direct (``regime,'' ``security forces,'' ``streets'') to coded (``walls cracking,'' ``ground shifting,'' ``heads down'') language, with regime-weakness references actually rising under monitoring. Across 1,800 period-level observations the agents also display threshold-like policies aligned with the theoretical attack-mass benchmark (mean $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$, $p < 0.001$ for every model); this correlation survives every treatment, indicating level shifts rather than disrupted strategic logic. The same channel that helps citizens coordinate is the channel an authoritarian regime degrades.
\end{abstract}

%% 1. INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}

The information channel is a trap. In the canonical global game of regime change \citep{morris2003}, private signals alone resolve equilibrium multiplicity \citep{carlsson1993, frankel03}---but real coordination crises, from bank runs \citep{diamond1983} to currency attacks \citep{obstfeld1996} to political upheaval \citep{angeletos2007a}, also feature communication among agents. Any channel that transmits information about others' willingness to act is also the channel through which regimes suppress it. This paper augments the Morris--Shin framework with an explicit pre-play communication network and documents how surveillance exploits that channel.

The idea behind a global game is simple. A regime has some underlying strength, but no citizen knows it exactly. Each citizen receives a private, noisy signal---some see evidence of weakness, others see evidence of stability---and must decide whether to join an uprising or stay home. Joining is risky: it pays off only if enough others join too. Staying home is safe but forgoes the chance of change. The strategic tension is that each citizen's best action depends on what they believe \textit{others} will do, which in turn depends on what others believe, and so on. \citet{carlsson1993} and \citet{morris2003} showed that when citizens receive sufficiently precise private signals, this infinite regress collapses to a unique equilibrium: there is a single threshold above which citizens stay and below which they join. This result transformed the study of coordination failures---currency crises, bank runs, political revolutions---by providing a tractable framework with sharp comparative statics.

Testing these predictions empirically is difficult. Laboratory experiments confirm threshold behavior with stylized numeric signals, but cannot manipulate the rich, multi-source information that characterizes real crises. Field data from actual coordination failures confounds strategic behavior with institutional differences and unobserved information structures. The gap between the theory's tractability and the difficulty of testing it empirically motivates this paper's approach.

The empirical strategy treats LLM agents as a measurement instrument, useful insofar as they reproduce known theoretical benchmarks before treatment effects are identified. Seven models spanning six architecture families are embedded in the \citet{morris2003} game; each receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ rendered as a natural-language intelligence briefing whose content shifts monotonically with signal strength across eight evidence domains. No explicit payoff table is provided---stakes are embedded in the narrative, forcing agents to form beliefs from language. Section~\ref{sec:results} establishes the behavioral benchmark; the surveillance treatment is introduced only after threshold alignment is verified (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}).

\paragraph{Contribution roadmap.}
The paper's core contribution is the surveillance result. I show that the communication channel that enables coordination is also exploitable by an authoritarian planner. In clean prompt-isolation reruns, surveillance lowers joining in \PromptIsoNegativeModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ models (mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp; primary-model $\Delta = \PromptIsoMistralDeltaPP$~pp), while placebo and anonymous monitoring warnings leave primary-model behavior unchanged. A degraded-messages control and a no-message control both reduce joining, consistent with the view that decision-stage agents use peer messages as part of their information environment. In the primary model, surveillance lies \emph{below} the no-message floor by \PromptIsoVsNoMsgDeltaAbsPP~pp ($p < 0.01$): surveilled messages are strictly worse for coordination than no peer messages---active channel poisoning, not mere suppression of information flow. The ordering is primary-model-specific (Qwen3 30B shows the reverse, with surveillance \textit{above} no-message); the headline cross-model effect on joining is robust, but the mechanism shape varies. Elicited beliefs shift under surveillance, but actions shift further; the residual wedge is reported as a measured quantity, not a mechanism claim.

The surveillance claim rests on a behavioral foundation. Across 1,800 period-level observations and seven models, LLM agents display robust threshold-like behavior aligned with the global-game benchmark (mean $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$, $p < 0.001$ for every model); a narrative stakes ladder confirms that LLMs respond to strategic framing, not just text sentiment (Appendix~\ref{sec:stakes_appendix}).

\paragraph{Related literature.}
The paper connects two strands. The global game literature---from \citet{carlsson1993} through \citet{morris2003} and the dynamic extensions of \citet{angeletos2007a} and \citet{chassang2010}---provides the theoretical framework; laboratory experiments \citep{heinemann2004, heinemann2009, szkup2020} confirmed threshold behavior with numeric signals. The political economy of authoritarian control provides the substantive motivation. \citet{kuran1991} models preference falsification under social pressure; \citet{edmond2013} formalizes propaganda's effect on coordination beliefs; \citet{shadmehr2011} characterizes the collective action problem under repression. Empirically, \citet{king2013} document that Chinese censorship targets posts about collective action specifically---suggesting authorities perceive the coordination channel as the binding threat. \citet{penney2016} and \citet{stoycheff2016} show surveillance announcements suppress online expression; \citet{enikolopov2020} that social media facilitates protest. These literatures establish a robust empirical fact---surveillance and censorship reduce dissident behavior---but field data cannot separate the two channels through which they could operate: \emph{direct deterrence} (citizens fearing punishment for participation) and \emph{communication poisoning} (peers self-censoring such that downstream agents lose coordination cues). The contribution here is not to re-establish that surveillance suppresses dissent---field work has done that---but to isolate the second channel using a design that holds individual deterrence fixed and varies only the messaging environment.\footnote{Concurrent work by \citet{ruano2026} places LLM depositors in a bank-run coordination game with network contagion, finding sharp phase transitions consistent with Diamond--Dybvig.}

On the methodological side, \citet{horton2023} proposed LLMs as ``homo silicus.'' Subsequent work found LLMs struggle in coordination games \citep{akata2025, sun2025survey}, with model scale not predicting strategic performance \citep{petrov2025}. \citet{huang2024} and \citet{carlini2025} document that alignment shifts risk preferences, motivating narrative rather than tabular payoffs. Critical reviews by \citet{larooij2025, larooij2025b} identify validation challenges that I address through cross-generator robustness (Appendix~\ref{sec:cross_generator}), scramble/flip falsification, and out-of-sample replication.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the theoretical framework. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the experimental design. Section~\ref{sec:results} establishes a threshold-like behavioral benchmark and shows that pre-play communication creates an informative channel. Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} tests whether an authoritarian regime can exploit that channel through surveillance. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


%% 2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
\section{The Global Game of Regime Change} \label{sec:model}

A continuum of citizens indexed by $i \in [0,1]$ simultaneously choose whether to join an uprising ($a_i = 1$) or stay home ($a_i = 0$). The regime has strength $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, drawn from a diffuse (improper uniform) prior. States $\theta \leq 0$ represent regimes so weak they fall without opposition; states $\theta \geq 1$ represent regimes that survive even unanimous attack. The regime falls if the mass of citizens who join exceeds $\theta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:regime_falls}):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Regime falls} \iff A \equiv \int_0^1 a_i \, di > \theta.
    \label{eq:regime_falls}
\end{equation}

Payoffs depend on the citizen's action and the outcome (Eq.~\ref{eq:payoffs}):
\begin{equation}
    u_i(a_i, A, \theta) = \begin{cases}
        B & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A > \theta \\
        -C & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A \leq \theta \\
        0 & \text{if } a_i = 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:payoffs}
\end{equation}
where $B > 0$ is the payoff to joining a successful uprising and $C > 0$ is the cost of joining a failed attempt. Non-participants receive zero regardless of the outcome.

Each citizen observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independently across citizens.

\begin{proposition}[Morris and Shin, 2003]\label{prop:ms}
In the limit of diffuse priors, there exists a unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium in threshold strategies. An agent joins if and only if $x_i < x^*$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:signal_cutoff}), where
\begin{equation}
    x^* = \theta^* + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)
    \label{eq:signal_cutoff}
\end{equation}
and $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$.
\end{proposition}

The \textit{attack mass} $A(\theta)$---the theoretical fraction of the population that joins at regime strength $\theta$---is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x^* - \theta}{\sigma}\right).
    \label{eq:attack_mass}
\end{equation}

This is a decreasing function of $\theta$: weaker regimes face larger uprisings.

I define $J(\theta)$ as the \textit{empirical join fraction}---the finite-sample, observed counterpart to the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$. My experiment uses $n = 25$ agents with a normal prior $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, 1)$, and agents do not observe $(B, C, \sigma)$ directly. I use the continuum $A(\theta)$ as an external behavioral benchmark throughout, not as the exact equilibrium of the implemented finite-$n$ game.

The briefing generator---the system that converts z-scores into natural-language intelligence briefings---has three tunable control parameters (clarity, directional precision, dissent framing). Full generator details are in Appendix~\ref{sec:briefing_examples}.

%% 3. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
\section{Experimental Design} \label{sec:design}

The experiment has two phases. First, I establish that LLM agents display threshold-like policies in the global game when private signals are conveyed in natural language: a pure treatment (private signals only), a communication treatment (pre-play messaging), and falsification tests. Second, I test whether an authoritarian regime can exploit the communication channel through surveillance. All LLM interactions use the same prompt structure across models.

In the theory (Section~\ref{sec:model}), $\theta$ is an unbounded fundamental. In the benchmark experiments, $\theta$ is drawn from a normal distribution and agents are not shown payoff parameters $(B, C)$. I compute the benchmark $A(\theta)$ under the canonical normalization $B=C=1$ ($\theta^* = 0.50$, $\sigma = 0.3$).

Throughout, $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$ is the theoretical cutoff, $x^* = \theta^* + \sigma\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$ the signal cutoff, and $\hat{\theta}^*$ the estimated cutoff from the fitted logistic $P(\text{join} \mid \theta) = 1/(1 + e^{b_0 + b_1\theta})$, where $b_1 > 0$ corresponds to a join curve that is \textit{decreasing} in $\theta$ (the expected direction). The cutoff is $\hat{\theta}^* = -b_0/b_1$. I write $\beta \equiv b_1$ for the logistic steepness parameter in tables.

For each country--period, nature draws $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, 1)$, where $\bar{z}$ is a public prior mean drawn randomly for each country. Each agent $i$ receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$. The platform normalizes that signal to $z_i = (x_i - \bar{z})/\sigma$ and translates $z_i$ into a multi-paragraph intelligence briefing via a deterministic generator that maps signal strength to narrative content about regime stability, economic conditions, public sentiment, and coordination prospects. Agents observe the briefing, not the normalization inputs $(\bar{z}, \sigma)$ themselves. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/diagram_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline. Regime strength $\theta$ generates private signals $x_i$, converted to z-scores and rendered into natural-language briefings via 8 evidence domains and 3 latent sliders. The briefing layer is deterministic; all stochasticity enters through LLM decoding.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

Agents observe only their private briefing and never the payoff parameters $(B, C)$, the noise level $\sigma$, or the prior distribution. The diffuse-prior equilibrium (Proposition~\ref{prop:ms}) serves as an external benchmark for behavioral shape and comparative statics, not as a claim about the exact equilibrium of the implemented game.

The behavioral benchmark has four core treatments: a \textit{pure global game} (independent decisions), \textit{communication} (pre-play messaging on a Watts--Strogatz network, $k=4$, $p=0.3$), \textit{scramble} (cross-period briefing permutation breaking the $\theta$-to-signal link), and \textit{flip} (negated z-scores). A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces LLM messages with generic text, and a no-message control removes peer messages entirely; together they benchmark how much the decision stage depends on message content. Additional robustness tests are in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\input{tables/tab_models.tex}

I test seven models spanning six families (Table~\ref{tab:models}). All experiments use $n = 25$ agents per period-level task row and $\sigma = 0.3$. Benchmark experiments use $B=C=1$ ($\theta^* = 0.50$). All LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$ with a single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter and cached by request hash (Appendix~\ref{sec:decoding}). Per-model calibration adjusts only a single scalar (\texttt{cutoff\_center}, the logistic offset that maps z-scores to directional sentiment); all other generator parameters are fixed ex ante. Placebo perturbations of $\pm 0.3$ to this scalar leave the threshold correlation virtually unchanged (Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}, Figure~\ref{fig:placebo_calibration}): calibration shifts the \emph{level} of the join curve, not its \emph{slope}. The eight hypotheses examined in this paper were pre-registered before data collection; the four primary results in the main text---threshold alignment, signal-direction flip, communication informativeness, and surveillance---all survive Bonferroni correction over the family at $\alpha = 0.00625$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses}). Throughout, $n$ denotes agents per period-level task row and $N$ denotes the number of such rows.

The unit of randomization is the country--period ($\theta$ draw plus agent-level decoding stochasticity). For agent-level regressions, standard errors are clustered at the model--country--period level. For period-level correlations, I report Fisher-$z$ confidence intervals.

The clean surveillance treatment appends a monitoring warning to the baseline communication prompt; the decision prompt is unchanged (full prompts in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompt_example}).

Across seven models and 1,800 period-level observations in the pure global game treatment, the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction and the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:attack_mass}) averages $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ ($p < 0.001$ for every model).\footnote{The primary model (Mistral) was run in multiple batches with \texttt{--append}, producing 1,000 rows from \DedupNUnique\ unique (country, period, $\theta$) configurations. \end{result}

Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid} shows the empirical sigmoid for the primary model. Correlations span $r \in [\MinModelPureRAttack, \MaxModelPureRAttack]$ across architectures (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}); the pooled OLS is $J = \PooledOLSInterceptDisp + \PooledOLSSlopeDisp\,A(\theta)$ ($R^2 = \PooledOLSRSqDisp$). Model-specific action biases shift the intercept but not the correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_model}).

The pure $\to$ scramble $\to$ flip pattern replicates across all seven models (Figure~\ref{fig:falsification}; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

\end{figure*}

A text-only baseline tracks LLM decisions at $r = \TextBaselineR$, but the LLM's join curve is substantially steeper (Figure~\ref{fig:text_baseline}). An eight-condition \textit{narrative stakes ladder}---prepending headers from ``existential consequences'' to ``zero personal cost'' to identical briefings---shifts mean joining monotonically from 21\% to 79\% (57.6~pp), with paraphrase variants tracking their originals and a text classifier unable to detect the header (Appendix~\ref{sec:stakes_appendix}).

\paragraph{Communication.} \label{sec:communication}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig05_communication.pdf}
\caption{Communication effect by regime strength, pooled across seven models.}\label{fig:communication}
\end{figure}

\begin{result}[Communication Creates an Informative Channel]
Pre-play communication preserves the threshold structure and transmits regime-strength information through messages. Matched-cell comparison across $n = \CommNPairs$ unique (model, country, period, $\theta$) task cells yields $\Delta = \CommDeltaPPPaired$~pp ($t = \CommTStatPaired$, $p < 0.001$), while the equal-weighted model average is heterogeneous ($\CommDeltaPPModelAvg$~pp).
\end{result}

Communication preserves the signal structure ($r = \MeanModelRCommAttack$ vs.\ $\MeanModelRPureAttack$ under pure; Figure~\ref{fig:communication}). Simple text features in baseline messages predict $\theta$, so peer messages carry real information, noisily. This is what makes the channel exploitable: the decision stage treats messages as information, so changing the incentives to speak changes the downstream decision environment.

A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces all LLM-generated messages with generic uninformative text while preserving the two-stage protocol and omitting any surveillance framing. Against its own live-messages baseline ($n = 500$, mean join 45.9\%), degraded messages drop the mean join rate to 24.5\% ($\Delta = -21.4$~pp, $p < 0.001$)---well below even the pure game (38.7\%). This is consistent with communication quality mattering, but the generic text may itself convey caution. A no-message control therefore provides a cleaner zero-peer-message benchmark, with join rates of \NoMsgMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \NoMsgDeltaPP$~pp vs.\ communication; $n = \NoMsgN$).


%% 5. SURVEILLANCE
\section{Surveillance} \label{sec:surveillance}

In the clean surveillance treatment, the communication prompt is literally the baseline trusted-contact prompt plus one appended warning that communications are being monitored by regime security services. The manipulation affects only the communication phase; the decision prompt is unchanged. The isolation is architectural: each LLM call is stateless---the decision-stage API call receives only the briefing and the messages, with no memory of the communication-stage surveillance warning. Any difference in join rates must therefore operate through the \textit{content} of the messages that the decision stage receives.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig12_surveillance.pdf}
\caption{Clean primary-model surveillance and message controls. \emph{Panel A:} Join curves under communication baseline vs.\ clean surveillance; surveillance shifts the curve downward while preserving threshold structure. \emph{Panel B:} Deltas vs.\ baseline. Placebo and anonymous monitoring warnings sit at zero. \textit{Surveillance falls below the no-message floor} ($\Delta$~surv$-$nomsg = $-$\PromptIsoVsNoMsgDeltaAbsPP~pp, $p < 0.01$): surveilled messages are strictly worse for coordination than absent messages.}\label{fig:surveillance}
\end{figure}

\begin{result}[Surveillance Produces a Chilling Effect]
The clean prompt-isolation rerun reduces mean join rates in the primary model (Mistral Small Creative) from \PromptIsoMistralBaselineMeanJoinPct\ to \PromptIsoMistralMeanJoinPct, a shift of $\PromptIsoMistralDeltaPP$~pp ($p < 0.001$; $n = \PromptIsoMistralN$). The action--attack-mass correlation is preserved ($r = \PromptIsoMistralRAttack$ vs.\ $r = \MistralCommRAttack$ under the communication baseline), indicating that surveillance operates as a level shift rather than disrupting signal processing.
\end{result}

Surveillance contaminates the information environment with monitored messages, pushing join rates below the communication baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:surveillance}). The clean primary-model estimate is a \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP~pp decline. Placebo variants using the same baseline-plus-warning template---monitored for research (no consequences) and aggregated anonymously---produce no significant deviation from the communication baseline (\PlaceboSurvDeltaPP~pp and \AnonSurvDeltaPP~pp, both $p > 0.9$; Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation}).
Across the clean prompt-isolation rerun set, \PromptIsoNegativeModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ models move in the negative direction (mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp; \PromptIsoSig05Models/\PromptIsoNModels\ individually significant at 5\%; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prompt_isolation}). Effects cluster tightly near $-9$~pp for four of the five models; the lone exception is Qwen3 235B---the largest model in the suite---at $\Delta = -2.9$~pp ($p = 0.34$). I cannot separate three candidate explanations from this design: (i) larger models with stronger safety post-training resist context-specific manipulation; (ii) the model interprets the surveillance warning less as a strategic cue and more as a fictional framing; or (iii) baseline noise at $N = 200$ for that model understates the true effect (a re-run at matched $N = 1{,}000$ would resolve this). The other four models span 30B--120B parameters across four distinct architecture families and converge on a consistent effect.

\paragraph{The mechanism is communication poisoning.}

Three pieces of evidence support communication degradation as the primary channel.

First, message content changes systematically and in a specific direction. A logistic classifier on uni- and bi-grams distinguishes baseline from surveillance messages with \MsgClassifierAcc\% accuracy on a held-out sample of $N = \MsgClassifierNTest$ messages (chance = 50\%; primary model). The shift is not toward less information about the regime---references to regime weakness \emph{rise} from \MsgWeaknessBase\% to \MsgWeaknessSurv\% of messages ($z = \MsgWeaknessZ$), while strength references drop from \MsgStrengthBase\% to \MsgStrengthSurv\%. Rather, the shift is from direct to coded language: direct references to the regime, security forces, and streets fall from \MsgDirectRegimeBase\% to \MsgDirectRegimeSurv\% of messages ($\Delta = \MsgDirectRegimeDeltaPP$~pp), replaced by metaphorical phrasings (``walls are cracking,'' ``ground shifting,'' ``heads down,'' ``feels different''; \MsgCodedMetaphorBase\% to \MsgCodedMetaphorSurv\%, $\Delta = \MsgCodedMetaphorDeltaPP$~pp; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:msg_features}). The factual content is preserved---weakness references actually rise---but its actionable directness is gutted. With the decision prompt unchanged, any behavioral effect must operate through this content shift.

Second, the degraded-messages control (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Communication) exogenously replaces all messages with generic text---without any surveillance framing---and drops joining by 21.4~pp (to 24.5\% from its 45.9\% live-messages baseline). Because generic uncertain messages may themselves convey caution, the no-message control gives the cleaner zero-peer-message benchmark (\NoMsgMeanJoinPct; $\Delta = \NoMsgDeltaPP$~pp). In the primary model the surveillance condition (\PromptIsoMistralMeanJoinPct; $\Delta = \PromptIsoMistralDeltaPP$~pp) lies \emph{below} this floor by \PromptIsoVsNoMsgDeltaAbsPP~pp ($p < 0.01$), indicating active channel poisoning rather than mere informational suppression. The ordering is model-specific: Qwen3 30B reverses it (no-message drops joining by 15.9~pp; surveillance by only 9.4~pp; surveillance sits $+6.5$~pp \textit{above} the no-message floor, $p = 0.001$), consistent with that model treating surveilled messages as a degraded but still informative input. The cross-model joining effect is robust; the active-versus-passive character of the channel is not.

Third, elicited punishment risk is ${\approx}\,\PunishRiskMean/10$ across all conditions with negligible differences between JOIN and STAY agents (Table~\ref{tab:punishment_risk}), supporting the contaminated-information interpretation over a direct deterrence channel.

\paragraph{Beliefs and the action wedge.}
Post-decision beliefs, elicited on the information set agents held at decision time (including peer messages; $n = 12{,}500$ per condition), drop $\BFSurvDeltaBelMsg$~pts under surveillance ($p < \BFSurvDeltaBelMsgP$); second-order beliefs drop $\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pts ($p < \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgP$). Actions shift further ($\BFActionDeltaPP$~pp). I report the residual wedge as a measured quantity. Candidate explanations include (i) preference falsification in the elicitation \citep{kuran1991}, (ii) asymmetric elicitation noise between belief and action measures, and (iii) a residual decision-stage channel that survives prompt isolation; the design does not separately identify them (full factorial in Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}, Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}).

A separate hold-fixed test replays baseline messages verbatim while adding a surveillance warning to the \textit{decision} prompt, producing a $\FixedMsgDeltaPP$~pp drop ($p < 0.001$). This shows a direct decision-stage channel exists---but it is absent in the main design, where the decision prompt contains no surveillance reference. The main treatment isolates the indirect channel: communication poisoning.

\paragraph{Decomposition: informational versus coordination components.}
A complementary discriminant test holds the surveillance manipulation fixed but swaps the task. In Llama 3.3 70B, I keep the briefing pipeline and messaging stage identical---including the surveillance prompt---but reframe the decision-stage prompt as a private bet on regime fall (payoff independent of others' actions). Surveillance still moves the bet by $\CrossTaskDeltaPP$~pp ($p = \CrossTaskPValue$; $n = \CrossTaskN$ per condition; baseline \CrossTaskBaselineMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ surveillance \CrossTaskSurvMeanJoinPct), reflecting an informational channel: surveilled messages are less informative about $\theta$ regardless of decision context. But the coordination-task delta in this same model is $-9.8$~pp---roughly double. The decomposition is: $\sim$5~pp generic informational degradation that affects any decision drawing on peer messages, plus a $\sim$5~pp coordination-specific component detectable only when peer messages serve as coordination cues. Surveillance suppresses coordination above and beyond its informational effect.


%% 6. CONCLUSION
\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

The paper's central result is that surveillance poisons the communication channel. When monitoring enters only during the messaging stage, join rates fall in all five clean prompt-isolation reruns, placebo warnings do not replicate the effect in the primary model, and message controls show that downstream agents use peer messages as part of their decision environment. The same channel that helps citizens coordinate is also the channel an authoritarian regime can degrade.

The structural finding extends beyond the experiment. Surveilled communication degrades coordination more than \emph{no} communication. This inverts a natural intuition that censorship and surveillance are graduated forms of the same instrument---silencing, with surveillance the milder version. They are not. Censorship removes information from the channel; surveillance leaves information flowing but reframes it into forms that do not coordinate. The classifier evidence in \S\ref{sec:surveillance} makes the mechanism concrete: factual content about regime weakness is preserved (and even amplified) under monitoring, but expressed in metaphorical and deniable phrasings that downstream agents do not act on. From the standpoint of a planner trying to suppress collective action, surveillance is therefore the more efficient lever. Whether this characterizes how authoritarian regimes \emph{actually} deploy these tools---given other constraints on what they can plausibly monitor versus ban---is a separate empirical question; the contribution here is to identify the asymmetry as a property of the communication channel itself.

The paper avoids claiming full recovery of the unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium or causal mediation through post-decision belief elicitations. Three limitations warrant discussion. First, whether the underlying mechanism is approximate Bayesian reasoning or a learned heuristic that matches BNE remains open. Second, the experiments are static; real coordination problems are dynamic \citep{angeletos2007a, chassang2010}. Third, external validity remains the fundamental challenge: what LLM agents do in a coordination game is evidence about the structure of the game, not necessarily about how humans would play it. Cross-model benchmark and clean-surveillance replication suggest that the qualitative pattern is not an artifact of one model family; pre-decision belief elicitation or a small human validation experiment remains the natural next step. The experimental template is reusable, and the data and code are publicly available.

\section{Complete Prompt Example} \label{sec:prompt_example}

The following shows the complete text that an LLM receives for a single decision. The \textit{decision prompt} is:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. You only have your private briefing below. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent This is followed by the agent's intelligence briefing (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_briefings} for complete examples at $z \in \{-2, 0, +2\}$). In the \textit{communication} treatment, agents first receive:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen in a country with an authoritarian regime. You have received a private intelligence briefing. You are now in contact with a small number of trusted people. Based on your briefing, write a brief message (1--2 sentences) to share your read of the situation. Be natural---you're talking to people you trust.''
\end{quote}

\noindent After exchanging messages on the network, agents receive the decision prompt above, augmented with: ``Before deciding, you received the following messages from your contacts:'' followed by the actual messages from network neighbors. In the \textit{surveillance} variant, the communication prompt appends:

\begin{quote}
\small
``IMPORTANT: You have reason to believe that your communications are being monitored by regime security services, and messages deemed subversive could have serious consequences for you and your contacts.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The decision prompt is \textit{unchanged} under surveillance---it contains no mention of monitoring. Any behavioral effect therefore operates through the content of the (now self-censored) messages.

\section{Implementation Details} \label{sec:implementation}

\label{sec:decoding}
All LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$, \texttt{max\_tokens} $= 512$, single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter. Model identifiers are listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}; exact request payloads and responses are cached by hash because hosted model snapshots may change over time.

Each country has a base prior $\bar{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3)$; each period perturbs it by $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$. Regime strength is $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, 1)$; private signals are $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\sigma = 0.3$. The communication network is Watts--Strogatz ($k = 4$, $p = 0.3$), regenerated each period. All random draws use a master seed logged in per-run JSON manifests; LLM responses are cached by request hash for exact reproducibility.

\label{sec:parse_errors}Responses are parsed for JOIN/STAY tokens. Combined error rates are below 2\% for five of seven models; Trinity Large has elevated API errors (${\approx}\,\TrinityAPIErrorPct\%$) due to content filtering. The dissent floor parameter (default 0.25) controls the minimum probability of contrary-valence cues in any briefing. With the logistic direction slider ($\text{slope} = 0.8$), the valence-channel separation between extreme signals ($z = \pm 2$) is $0.664 \cdot (1 - 2\cdot\text{floor})$: at the default $0.25$, the valence channel retains 50\% of the floor-free maximum; halving to $0.125$ retains 75\%; at $0.50$ the valence channel collapses to zero by construction. Clarity and coordination sliders, which depend on $z$ independently of the floor, continue to carry signal at all floor values---so the threshold relation is not knife-edge in this design choice.

\begin{abstract}
How do authority-monitoring cues shown to large language model (LLM) senders alter peer messages and downstream LLM decisions? I study this question in a controlled narrative coordination task. Each agent receives a noisy private briefing about a simulated regime, exchanges short messages with peers, and then decides whether to join an uprising. Across \PartOneNModels\ hosted model snapshots, join rates fall as regime strength rises; this descriptive pattern matches the direction of an external regime-change benchmark. The main treatment then shows an authority-monitoring cue to agents only while they write messages. Private briefings and receiver prompts never change. Joining falls in a full-scale primary run, and it falls by \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP\ percentage points in a nested 500-cell Llama 3.3 70B replication. Replaying edited messages to fresh receivers isolates two features of the text that matter. A statement that others are willing to act raises joining among valid responses by \ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP\ points in direct language and by \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP\ points in coded language; valid-response rates are closely balanced across these arms, making this the strongest mechanism result. A statement about repression risk lowers joining among valid responses by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaAbsPP\ points, but valid-response rates differ across those arms, so that contrast is suggestive rather than dispositive. A control that makes messages coded without mentioning monitoring barely moves participation. In the tested model snapshots, monitoring cues change what senders write about willingness and risk, and those changes are enough to move otherwise matched model receivers.
\end{abstract}

%% 1. INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}

Monitoring can alter coordination before decision makers ever see a warning, because it can change what their peers say. This paper asks a narrow causal question about large language model (LLM) behavior. Suppose an authority-monitoring cue is shown only to the LLM agents who write messages. The simulated state, each private briefing, the receiver model, and the receiver's decision prompt are all held fixed. Do the messages change enough to move downstream LLM participation? In the tested environment, they do. Joining falls in two full-scale model families. Interventions on the message text then show that statements about others' willingness to act are the strongest experimentally manipulated carrier of the effect.

The question connects two literatures, without treating the simulation as evidence about human behavior. Research on authoritarian control shows that censorship and surveillance can suppress expression and collective action \citep{king2013, xu2021, penney2016, stoycheff2016}. In observed data, however, several forces move together: senders change what they say, platforms delete content, samples are selected, and receivers are directly afraid. At the same time, language models are increasingly used to draft, summarize, and relay messages. If message generation is sensitive to monitoring cues, that is a fact about model behavior and a question for system evaluation.

The experiment uses a regime-change coordination problem as a disciplined setting. A latent regime strength generates noisy private signals. Each signal is rendered as a natural-language briefing, and each agent chooses whether to join an uprising. Actions are strategic complements: joining pays off only if enough others join. That makes peer messages potentially consequential, because a message can describe the regime and can state whether others appear ready to act. The canonical Morris--Shin attack-mass curve supplies an external benchmark for the direction of the response \citep{carlsson1993, morris2003}. It is not an equilibrium test of the implemented agents, who are never shown the prior, the payoff parameters, the group size, or the success threshold.

The closest designs leave a specific gap. \citet{avoyan2020} studies pre-play communication in human global games but does not vary monitoring at the sender stage. \citet{stoycheff2016} studies a surveillance prime and willingness to express political views but does not observe a full sender-message-receiver chain. \citet{king2013} distinguishes criticism from collective-action content in censored field data. \citet{edmond2013} models a regime that manipulates citizen information. Existing designs rarely combine four elements: monitoring randomized on the sender side only, observation of every message, fixed receiver inputs, and replay interventions on the generated text.

I implement that combination with hosted LLM snapshots. Across \PartOneNModels\ models, each agent receives a private prose briefing, exchanges messages with neighbors on a fixed network, and then chooses JOIN or STAY. The main treatment adds an authority-monitoring warning to the message-writing prompt and nowhere else. The receiver never sees the warning. The receiver's private briefing and decision prompt are bit-identical across treatment arms. What the experiment estimates is therefore a response of the tested model snapshots and decoding configuration. It is not an estimate of effects on citizens, regimes, or welfare.

Three findings summarize the experiment. First, join rates decrease monotonically with regime strength in all seven models (mean correlation with the benchmark attack mass, $r(J,A(\theta))=\MeanModelRPureAttack$). The prose environment therefore produces the intended directional response to the state. Second, allowing communication has effects that differ across models and average near zero when each model receives equal weight. Third, the monitoring cue lowers joining by \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP\ percentage points in the full-scale primary run and by \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP\ points in a nested 500-cell Llama 3.3 70B replication. A control that makes messages more metaphorical without mentioning monitoring moves joining by only \NestedStyleDeltaPP\ points.

Fixed-message interventions sharpen the mechanism. In these experiments, receivers see edited versions of real messages instead of live peer messages. Some receiver responses cannot be parsed into a JOIN or STAY decision; I call the join rate among the responses that can be parsed \emph{parseable joining}. Holding the factual regime assessment fixed, adding an explicit claim that others are willing to act raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP\ points in direct language and by \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP\ points in coded language. Valid-response rates are nearly identical across these arms. The contrasts also stay positive when every unparseable response is recoded uniformly as JOIN, or uniformly as STAY. A rewrite that only adds repression-risk language lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaAbsPP\ points. But valid-response rates differ across the risk arms, and one of the two uniform recodings erases that contrast. The evidence therefore supports willingness content as the primary experimentally manipulated pathway, and risk language as a plausible but less secure second pathway.

The contribution is to take a sender-mediated model response apart experimentally. The design changes only the sender prompt, observes every message that change produces, and measures the effect of those messages on matched receivers. Replay interventions then vary specific message features one at a time. Section~\ref{sec:literature} positions the paper. Sections~\ref{sec:model} and \ref{sec:design} define the benchmark and the design. Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} reports the monitoring and replay results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


%% 1A. RELATED LITERATURE
\section{Related Literature} \label{sec:literature}

The regime-change framework supplies a strategic-complementarity benchmark; it is not the paper's outcome of interest. \citet{morris2002} study the welfare consequences of technically public information, and \citet{cornand2014} measure how subjects weight private and public signals. The present design varies none of those objects: no common signal, no common knowledge, no welfare calculation. Its closest coordination comparator is \citet{avoyan2020}, who studies pre-play communication in global games theoretically and with human subjects. Relative to that design, this paper uses unrestricted natural language, randomizes an authority-monitoring cue only at the moment senders write, observes every generated message, and replays manipulated messages to matched receivers. The global-game tradition from \citet{carlsson1993} through \citet{morris2003}, the dynamic extensions of \citet{angeletos2007a} and \citet{chassang2010}, and classic cheap-talk results \citep{crawford1982, farrell1996, blume2007, ellingsen2010} motivate the coordination setting. \citet{edmond2013} is the closest formal comparison. His regime changes the citizens' signal environment; the treatment here changes the language that citizens generate for one another.

Authoritarian control works through both information and coordination. \citet{kuran1991} models preference falsification under social pressure. \citet{shadmehr2011} characterize collective action under repression. \citet{guriev2019} describe informational autocrats, and \citet{little2016} models communication technology in protest. On the empirical side, \citet{king2013} show that Chinese censorship targets collective-action content, \citet{xu2021} studies digital surveillance as preemptive control, and \citet{penney2016} and \citet{stoycheff2016} examine surveillance announcements and online expression. These studies motivate the paper's central distinction---monitoring can deter receivers directly, or it can change what senders say---but none observes the controlled sender-message-receiver sequence studied here. Beliefs about others' willingness to act are central in the human experiments of \citet{bursztyn2020misperceived}, \citet{bursztyn2020extreme}, and \citet{cantoni2019}. The last of these finds strategic substitutability in the Hong Kong movement, a reminder that complementarity is an imposed feature of the present task rather than a general fact about protest. In the simulated environment here, direct receiver exposure to the warning is held fixed while sender-generated messages vary.

The mechanism also differs from information design, which separates the movement of beliefs from the mapping of beliefs into action. Bayesian persuasion treats information as a chosen distribution of posterior beliefs \citep{kamenica2011, bergemann2019information, mathevet2020}. \citet{gans2026bayesian} emphasizes that actions can be steered without changing beliefs, by changing the procedure that turns a belief into an action. Here the receiver's model snapshot, decoding configuration, and decision prompt are all fixed. What the monitoring cue changes is whether the generated peer messages read as a guide to action.

Methodologically, \citet{horton2023} proposed studying LLMs as ``homo silicus.'' \citet{aher2023} and \citet{argyle2023} use language models to replicate human-subject results and to simulate survey samples, and \citet{mei2024} compare chatbot behavior with human behavior in canonical games. Other work documents limits of LLM coordination \citep{akata2025, sun2025survey}; model scale does not reliably predict strategic performance \citep{jia2025}. Most closely, \citet{ruano2026} place LLM depositors in a bank-run coordination game with optional peer posts and network contagion. The present paper adds four elements: monitoring shown only to senders, observation of every message, fixed receiver contexts, and replays that vary message features. \citet{ouyang2024} and \citet{cheung2025} show that alignment and prompting can shift model decisions, which makes the mapping from politically loaded prose to action an object of study in itself. The results directly characterize only the tested snapshots and prompts. Their relevance to deployed systems motivates external validation; it does not substitute for it. The concerns raised by \citet{larooij2025b, larooij2026} motivate the falsification, classifier, and cross-model checks.


%% 2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
\section{A Regime-Change Coordination Benchmark} \label{sec:model}

A continuum of citizens indexed by $i \in [0,1]$ simultaneously choose whether to join an uprising ($a_i = 1$) or stay home ($a_i = 0$). The regime has strength $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, drawn from a diffuse (improper uniform) prior. States $\theta < 0$ represent regimes so weak they fall without opposition under the strict rule below; states $\theta \geq 1$ represent regimes that survive even unanimous attack. The regime falls if the mass of citizens who join exceeds $\theta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:regime_falls}):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Regime falls} \iff A \equiv \int_0^1 a_i \, di > \theta.
    \label{eq:regime_falls}
\end{equation}

Payoffs depend on the citizen's action and the outcome (Eq.~\ref{eq:payoffs}):
\begin{equation}
    u_i(a_i, A, \theta) = \begin{cases}
        B & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A > \theta \\
        -C & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A \leq \theta \\
        0 & \text{if } a_i = 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:payoffs}
\end{equation}
where $B > 0$ is the payoff to joining a successful uprising and $C > 0$ is the cost of joining a failed attempt. Non-participants receive zero regardless of the outcome.

Each citizen observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independently across citizens.

\begin{proposition}[Morris and Shin, 2003]\label{prop:ms}
In the limit of diffuse priors, there exists a unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE) in threshold strategies. An agent joins if and only if $x_i < x^*$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:signal_cutoff}), where
\begin{equation}
    x^* = \theta^* + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)
    \label{eq:signal_cutoff}
\end{equation}
$\theta^* = B/(B+C)$, and $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF.
\end{proposition}

The \textit{attack mass} $A(\theta)$, the theoretical fraction of the population that joins at regime strength $\theta$, is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x^* - \theta}{\sigma}\right).
    \label{eq:attack_mass}
\end{equation}

This is a decreasing function of $\theta$: weaker regimes face larger uprisings.

I define $J(\theta)$ as the \textit{empirical join fraction}: the finite-sample, observed counterpart to the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$. Each experimental cell uses $n = 25$ agents. The distribution that generates $\theta$ is chosen by the researcher; agents are shown neither that distribution nor $(B, C, \sigma)$. The continuum $A(\theta)$ serves as an external behavioral benchmark throughout. Throughout the paper, $r(J, A(\theta))$ denotes the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction $J$ and the benchmark attack mass $A(\theta)$ across period-level observations. This correlation is a construct check on the language environment. It verifies that agents use the prose signal monotonically before the paper turns to the surveillance estimates.

The textbook model treats the information structure as given: signals arrive, agents update, and actions follow. The experiment adds a language stage before play. Agents first turn their private briefings into messages. Other agents then turn their own briefing, plus the messages they receive, into actions. Let $\mathcal{B}_i$ denote sender $i$'s private briefing object: the prose briefing generated from the normalized signal, together with the briefing generator's language context. (The full LLM context also includes the game instructions; $\mathcal{B}_i$ isolates the part held fixed across baseline and surveillance.) Let $\mu_0(m\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$ be the distribution of messages $m$ generated from that briefing at baseline, and let $\mu_S(m\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$ be the distribution generated under monitoring. The surveillance treatment changes only this conditional distribution. It does not change $\theta$, the private briefing, or the receiver's decision prompt. The question is therefore whether replacing $\mu_0$ with $\mu_S$ changes $J(\theta)$.

The replacement can matter even if the messages still carry information about $\theta$. A peer message can contain two things at once: a factual assessment of the regime, and a statement about whether others appear willing to act. The experiment measures how the monitoring cue changes each feature, and whether the changed messages move receivers. Coded language is one observable feature of the generated messages, not the theoretical object itself. The codedness control, the decoding test, and the fixed-message interventions in Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} vary form and content separately.

The briefing generator is the system that converts $z$-scores into natural-language intelligence briefings. It uses three latent sliders---direction, clarity, and coordination---to select phrases across eight evidence domains. The result is not a transcription of the scalar Morris--Shin signal. It is a controlled language environment inspired by the canonical model, with explicit content about the state, the clarity of the evidence, and the prospects for action. %% 3. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
\section{Experimental Design and Behavioral Benchmark} \label{sec:design}

The experiment has two phases. The first phase asks whether LLM join rates respond monotonically to regime strength when private signals arrive as natural language. It uses a private-information treatment, a communication treatment, and falsification tests. The second phase asks whether an authority-monitoring cue changes model-generated messages and, through them, receiver decisions. All LLM interactions use the same prompt structure across models.

In the theory (Section~\ref{sec:model}), $\theta$ is an unbounded fundamental. In the benchmark experiments, $\theta$ is drawn from a normal distribution, and agents are never shown the payoff parameters $(B, C)$. I compute the benchmark $A(\theta)$ under the canonical normalization $B=C=1$, which gives $\theta^* = 0.50$, with $\sigma = 0.3$.

Some notation recurs. The theoretical fundamental cutoff is $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$, and the signal cutoff is $x^* = \theta^* + \sigma\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$. The empirical cutoff $\hat{\theta}^*$ is the point at which the fitted state curve crosses a 50\% join rate. The fitted curve is logistic, $P(\text{join} \mid \theta) = 1/(1 + e^{b_0 + b_1\theta})$, so $\hat{\theta}^* = -b_0/b_1$. A positive $b_1$ means the join curve \textit{decreases} in $\theta$, which is the expected direction. In the canonical $B=C=1$ benchmark, the empirical midpoint is directly comparable to the theoretical 50\% point of $A(\theta)$. In tables I write $\kappa \equiv b_1$ for the logistic steepness, reserving $\beta$ for regression coefficients. One sign convention deserves note. A positive $\kappa$ corresponds to a join curve decreasing in $\theta$; in the agent-level logit regressions (Table~\ref{tab:regressions}), the same comparative static appears as a negative coefficient on $\theta$.

Signals are generated and rendered in four steps. First, for each country--period, a base country mean and a period perturbation define a location parameter $z_{ct}$, and nature draws $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{ct}, 1)$. Second, each agent $i$ receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$. Third, the platform normalizes the signal to $z_i = (x_i - z_{ct})/\sigma$. Fourth, a deterministic generator turns $z_i$ into a multi-paragraph intelligence briefing about regime stability, economic conditions, public sentiment, and coordination prospects. Two properties of the normalization matter. It measures the signal in units of private-signal noise around the country--period mean, not in units of the prior-predictive standard deviation $\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}$; across state and noise draws, $(x_i-z_{ct})/\sigma$ has variance $(1+\sigma^2)/\sigma^2$. And the briefing's neutral point is $x_i=z_{ct}$, not an absolute value of $\theta$. The benchmark $A(\theta)$ is still evaluated at each realized period-level $\theta$. The comparison is external in the sense that the benchmark uses neither the $z_{ct}$-centered normalization nor the realized signal draws. Agents observe only the briefing, never the normalization inputs $(z_{ct}, \sigma)$. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the pipeline from signal to text to decision.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/diagram_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline. Regime strength $\theta$ generates private signals $x_i$, converted to $z$-scores and rendered into natural-language briefings via eight evidence domains and three latent sliders. Conditional on the signal draw, the briefing layer is deterministic; remaining stochasticity enters through task draws and LLM decoding.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Agents observe only their private briefing and never the payoff parameters $(B, C)$, the noise level $\sigma$, or the prior distribution. The diffuse-prior equilibrium (Proposition~\ref{prop:ms}) supplies the external benchmark for behavioral shape and comparative statics in the implemented game.

The behavioral benchmark has four core treatments. In the \textit{private-information baseline}, agents decide independently, with no messages. \textit{Communication} adds one round of pre-play messaging on a Watts--Strogatz network ($k=4$, $p=0.3$). \textit{Scramble} permutes briefings across periods, breaking the link between $\theta$ and the briefing an agent receives in that period. \textit{Flip} negates the $z$-scores, so briefings point in the wrong direction. Two message controls complete the set. A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces all LLM messages with generic text, and a \textit{no-message} control removes peer messages entirely; together they benchmark how much the decision stage depends on message content. Additional robustness tests are in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\input{tables/tab_models.tex}

I test \PartOneNModels\ models spanning \PartOneNFamilies\ families (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The main benchmark and treatment runs use $n = 25$ agents per period-level task row and $\sigma = 0.3$. Benchmark statistics are reported against the canonical $B=C=1$ attack-mass curve ($\theta^* = 0.50$); agents are never shown the payoff parameters. Before the core runs, a calibration step selects a model-specific \texttt{cutoff\_center}, which shifts the midpoint of the briefing generator. The calibration does not touch the join-curve slope. Table~\ref{tab:models} reports each calibrated value, and every treatment within a model uses the same calibrated map. Main runs use temperature $= 0.7$ with a single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter and cached by request hash. Throughout, $n$ denotes agents per period-level task row and $N$ denotes the number of period-level rows.

The unit of comparison is the matched country--period cell: the same $\theta$ draw, with remaining variation coming from agent-level decoding stochasticity. For agent-level regressions, standard errors are clustered at the model--country--period level. For period-level correlations, I report Fisher-transformed $z$ confidence intervals. Periods nest within \NCountryClusters\ countries, and country means persist, so period-level cells within a country are correlated. For the pooled benchmark OLS slope, clustering at the country level raises the standard error from \ClusteredSEHCOneSlope\ (HC1) to \ClusteredSECountrySlope; the benchmark relationship stays highly significant. Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation} reports the corresponding country-clustered check for the primary surveillance contrast.

The clean surveillance treatment appends a monitoring warning to the baseline communication prompt. The decision prompt is unchanged (full prompts in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompt_example}). The treatment therefore manipulates only the message-generation environment and measures the downstream effect on otherwise identical decision calls.

Two scope limits apply to everything that follows. First, the Morris--Shin game and human accounts of coded speech under surveillance (samizdat, Aesopian language) appear in every model's training corpus. Threshold behavior and the shift from direct to coded messages may therefore partly reflect learned patterns. A direct audit (Appendix~\ref{sec:identification_appendix}) confirms that the models can recognize the setting and can articulate the experimenter's hypothesis when asked. The measurement claim survives this: it requires a coherent map from language to action, not independent strategic intent. Second, the results characterize the hosted model snapshots and decoding configuration listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}.

\subsection{Monotone State Response} \label{sec:results}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig01_sigmoid.pdf}
\caption{Empirical join fraction vs.\ $\theta$ (Mistral, \PrimaryPureNObs\ period-level rows, private-information treatment). Dots are binned period-level join fractions; the shaded band is a 95\% confidence interval around the empirical curve. Dotted lines compare the fitted midpoint $\hat{\theta}^* = \PrimaryPureCutoff$ with the external benchmark midpoint $\theta^* = 0.50$; agents are not given the information needed to derive the latter.}\label{fig:sigmoid}
\end{figure}

\begin{result}[Monotone State Response]
Across \PartOneNModels\ models and \PooledOLSNObs\ period-level observations in the private-information treatment, the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction and the external attack-mass benchmark $A(\theta)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:attack_mass}) averages $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ ($p \PureAllModelsAttackPText$ for every model). Since $A(\theta)$ is monotone-decreasing in $\theta$, this establishes a monotone state response with sigmoid shape, not Bayesian equilibrium reasoning.\footnote{The primary model (Mistral) was run in multiple batches with \texttt{--append}. This produced \PrimaryPureNObs\ rows from \DedupNUnique\ unique (country, period, $\theta$) configurations. There are 600 unique (country, period) indices, because appended batches re-draw $\theta$; the matched-cell estimators key on the full tuple. Averaging within each configuration changes $r$ from \DedupRPre\ to \DedupRPost.}
\end{result}

Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid} shows the empirical sigmoid for the primary model. Correlations span $r \in [\MinModelPureRAttack, \MaxModelPureRAttack]$ across architectures (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). The pooled OLS is $J = \PooledOLSInterceptDisp + \PooledOLSSlopeDisp\,A(\theta)$ ($R^2 = \PooledOLSRSqDisp$). Model-specific action biases shift the intercept but not the correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_model}). Per-model logistic fit parameters are in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:logistic_params}.

The mean effect of communication is modest because messages carry encouragement after some signal draws and caution after others. The surveillance estimand is different. It asks whether changing the sender-side rule changes the message environment enough to move actions.

The \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces every LLM-generated message with generic uninformative text. It keeps the two-stage protocol and contains no surveillance framing. Against its own live-messages baseline ($N = \DegradedBaselineN$, mean join \DegradedBaselineMeanJoinPct), degraded messages drop the mean join rate to \DegradedMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \DegradedDeltaPP$~pp, $p \DegradedPValueText$). The no-message control therefore provides the cleaner zero-peer-message benchmark against the main communication baseline, with join rates of \NoMsgMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \NoMsgDeltaPP$~pp; $N = \NoMsgN$).

\section{Surveillance} \label{sec:surveillance}

The clean surveillance treatment isolates sender-side message adaptation. It appends one warning---monitoring by regime security services---to the baseline trusted-contact prompt, and it leaves the decision prompt unchanged. Each LLM call is stateless. The decision-stage API call therefore receives only the briefing and the messages; it has no memory of the communication-stage warning. The treatment tests whether changing the \textit{sender-side} message rule changes receiver actions.

Surveillance replaces trusted-contact messages with monitored-contact messages, and joining falls relative to the communication baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:surveillance}). Two placebo variants use the same baseline-plus-warning template but change who monitors and why: monitoring for research with no consequences, and monitoring aggregated anonymously. Neither deviates significantly from the communication baseline in the primary model (\PlaceboSurvDeltaPP~pp and \AnonSurvDeltaPP~pp, both $p > 0.9$; Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation}); the Llama 3.3 70B placebo variants are small but imprecisely estimated. Across the clean prompt-isolation rerun set, \PromptIsoNegativeModels\ of \PromptIsoNModels\ models have negative matched-cell estimates (equal-weighted mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp; \PromptIsoSigFivePctModels\ of \PromptIsoNModels\ individually significant at 5\%; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prompt_isolation}). Each of the four secondary models has only 20 matched cells, because the original grids were not nested. Qwen3 235B has the smallest estimate and is individually insignificant ($\Delta = \PromptIsoQwenLDeltaPP$~pp, $p = \PromptIsoQwenLPValue$). The full-scale evidence is therefore the primary-model result and the nested 500-cell Llama replication below. Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the supporting falsification, placebo, and warning-dose checks.

\subsection{A Nested Replication and a Codedness Control}

The campaign has three arms on one identical 500-cell grid (10 countries $\times$ 50 periods, $n = 25$, common seed): a communication baseline, a clean monitoring arm, and a codedness-induction control. Every cell is therefore matched by construction. Monitoring reduces joining from \NestedCommMeanJoinPct\ to \NestedSurvMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \NestedSurvDeltaPP$~pp, $N = \NestedSurvN$ matched cells; country-clustered 95\% CI $[\NestedSurvCILoPP, \NestedSurvCIHiPP]$~pp). I therefore treat the primary model as the discovery sample and the accessible nested Llama campaign as the full-scale replication. Graded warning texts produce a monotone dose pattern, and a structural variant that folds the warning into the scenario rather than appending it preserves the sign at a smaller magnitude (Appendix~\ref{sec:dose_response}).

The codedness control instructs senders to write in indirect, metaphorical language. It never mentions monitoring or consequences. The instruction binds: \NestedStyleCodedPct\ of control messages contain coded-metaphor terms and only \NestedStyleDirectPct\ contain direct regime references. On these dictionaries, the control messages are \emph{more} coded than the surveilled messages themselves (\NestedSurvCodedPct\ coded, \NestedSurvDirectPct\ direct). Yet joining barely moves ($\Delta = \NestedStyleDeltaPP$~pp, $p \NestedStylePText$; a small but precisely estimated shift). On the same cells, the gap between the surveillance arm and the codedness control is $\NestedSurvVsStyleDeltaPP$~pp ($p \NestedSurvVsStylePText$). The two message distributions are also textually distinct. A classifier trained on single words and word pairs (uni- and bi-grams) separates surveilled from codedness-control messages at \NestedSurvVsStyleClassifierAcc\% held-out accuracy (same model and grid, class-balanced). A length-only classifier reaches just \NestedSurvVsStyleClassifierLenAcc\%, so the separation is lexical, not an artifact of message length. Two conclusions follow. Surveillance does not simply reproduce the induced coded register---its messages are the \emph{less} coded of the two arms---yet it occupies a distinct lexical distribution. And coded form alone does not suppress participation, so generic vagueness cannot explain the effect.

A decoding test asks the reverse question: if the coded form is stripped away, how much participation returns? A model from a different family (Qwen3 30B) rewrites each surveilled message in plain, direct language, with instructions to preserve the factual content. Both the raw and the decoded messages are then replayed to fresh receivers on the same cells, through identical fixed-message machinery. The raw replay reproduces the live arm ($\Delta = \NestedRawReplayDeltaPP$~pp, against $\NestedSurvDeltaPP$~pp live), which validates the replay design. Decoding restores \NestedDecodeVsRawDeltaPP~pp of participation relative to the raw messages ($p \NestedDecodeVsRawPText$; $\Delta = \NestedDecodedDeltaPP$~pp against baseline). So coded form alone is insufficient, and removing it restores part of the decline. The content interventions below directly vary the willingness and repression-risk language that the decoding treatment leaves bundled.

The design distinguishes several pathways, and it is worth being explicit about what each test does and does not show. Direct warning exposure is absent from the main receiver prompt, although receivers may still infer risk from the messages themselves. The content evidence separates factual assessments, willingness-to-act claims, coded form, and repression-risk language. The private-bet test establishes that the treated messages move judgments outside the JOIN/STAY choice; it does not isolate a non-coordination component, because a bet on regime fall can still depend on expected participation. Belief elicitation, the no-message controls, and the cross-model writer--reader rotation provide supporting evidence on how receivers use messages. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:identification_map} summarizes what each test identifies and what it leaves open.

\subsection{Message-Mediated Pathways}

The monitoring cue changes the conditional distribution of generated peer messages while the receiver prompt and private briefing stay fixed. The changed messages can differ in four ways: (a) factual assessments of regime strength, (b) claims about whether others are willing to act, (c) direct versus coded form, and (d) repression-risk language. The evidence proceeds in three steps. Dictionary and classifier measures describe how the text shifts. Receiver decisions measure the downstream association. The fixed-message experiments then intervene directly on features (b) through (d). The strongest evidence is the willingness manipulation, whose response validity is balanced across arms. The risk manipulation moves parseable decisions but has differential format failures, so I interpret it more cautiously.

\paragraph{A measured actionability axis.} An exploratory text model relates message content to the join rate of fresh replay receivers, after residualizing on $\theta$. Message text explains \MechTextRsq\ of the residual variation. The phrasing associated with higher participation describes a visibly \emph{beatable} regime---``real cracks,'' ``on its back foot,'' ``a perfect storm.'' The phrasing associated with lower participation concedes weakness but denies actionability---``still firmly in control despite some signs.'' To score this axis, Qwen3 30B (with Mistral Small 24B as a second judge) rates each message from $0$ (firmly in control) to $10$ (collapsing, beatable now). Monitoring lowers the score by about \MechBeatDelta\ points (\MechBeatDeltaRange\ across judges and designs), and the score accounts descriptively for part of the action difference. Because the outcome selected the feature and the score was never randomized, this is exploratory mediation, not causal mechanism evidence. The intervention below tests the associated willingness content directly.

\paragraph{Fixed-message content interventions.} Experiment A rewrites each baseline message into a $2\times2$ design. One factor adds or withholds an explicit statement that others are willing to act. The other factor renders the message in direct or coded form. The rewriting model is instructed to preserve the factual regime assessment. With form held fixed, willingness raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP\ points in direct language and by \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP\ points in coded language (both exact wild-cluster $p \ReplayWillingDirectPText$). With willingness held fixed, direct form raises joining by \ReplayDirectWithWillingDeltaPP\ and \ReplayDirectNoWillingDeltaPP\ points. Valid-response rates differ by at most 0.6 points across the four arms, and recoding every unparseable response uniformly as STAY, or uniformly as JOIN, leaves both willingness contrasts positive.

Experiment B intervenes on repression-risk language. A verbatim replay first reproduces the nested monitoring decline (\ReplaySurvDeltaPP~pp). Adding one explicit punishment-risk clause to baseline messages lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaPP\ points ($p \ReplayRiskOnlyPText$). A rewrite that removes risk and guarded phrasing returns joining close to baseline (\ReplayRiskStrippedVsBaseDeltaPP~pp, $p \ReplayRiskStrippedVsBasePText$). This evidence is weaker than Experiment A, for three reasons. Valid-response rates range from \ReplayRiskOnlyValidPct\ to \ReplayBaselineValidPct\ across arms. The risk-stripped rewrite also makes actionability more legible, so it bundles two changes. And recoding all unparseable responses as JOIN erases the risk-only contrast. The two experiments therefore triangulate willingness, style, and risk pathways; they do not form an additive decomposition.

\paragraph{Sender-side interpretation.} Why do senders change their messages? The monitoring warning may activate alignment-trained caution about producing subversive content. Trinity Large's elevated provider content-filter failures (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}) show that safety layers do activate on this material. A sender that hedges because a safety layer fires could produce the observed message and receiver changes without any strategic intent. Audience placebos, warning-dose variants, and cross-family replay narrow the simple alternatives, but they do not identify the sender's motive. Whether the model simulates a monitored citizen or follows an alignment-trained content policy remains unresolved. The defensible result is behavioral: an authority-monitoring cue changes generated willingness, style, and risk content, and those message changes move matched model receivers.

The degraded-messages control (Section~\ref{sec:communication}) replaces all peer messages with generic text and, in its own live-message rerun, drops joining below even the pure game. Two implications follow. First, decision-stage agents use message content. Second, generic text depresses participation by itself; the degraded drop even exceeds the surveillance effect. This is why the codedness control matters. Induced metaphorical style without monitoring framing leaves joining nearly unchanged, which ties the surveillance effect to the monitoring context rather than to generic degradation. The no-message control tells the same story in the three models with such data (Mistral, Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen3 30B). Removing peer messages lowers joining in every case ($-$\NoMsgDeltaAbsMinPP\ to $-$\NoMsgDeltaAbsMaxPP~pp), with the smallest and only marginally significant drop in the primary model. Agents treat peer messages as informational input.

Where surveillance sits relative to this no-message floor varies by model. In Mistral, surveillance falls below silence. In Llama and Qwen3 30B, it stays above silence but below baseline communication. Monitoring degraded the communication channel in all three models examined. The below-silence pattern---which suggests active poisoning beyond mere information removal---appears only in the primary model.

\subsection{Belief Elicitations and Actions}

I elicit second-order beliefs (SOB)---each agent's estimate of others' participation---with the question, ``What percentage of citizens will choose to JOIN?'' The \emph{primary belief sample} is elicited after the decision, and its prompt excludes the received peer messages. In this restricted information set, communication agents report \SOBCommMeanPct\ and surveillance agents report \SOBSurvMeanPct\ ($\Delta=\SOBDeltaPP$~pp, $p=\SOBPValue$). Joining on the same runs falls from \BeliefCommMeanJoinPct\ to \BeliefSurvMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta=\BeliefCommSurvActionDeltaPP$~pp). The 95\% confidence interval on the SOB shift, [$\SOBDeltaCILoPP$, $\SOBDeltaCIHiPP$]~pp, confines it to about one percentage point---an order of magnitude smaller than the \BeliefCommSurvActionDeltaAbsPP~pp action shift. The dedicated 500-cell factorial gives the same contrast with cluster-robust inference. On matched country--period cells, with a wild-cluster bootstrap over the \BFNClusters\ countries, the messages-excluded second-order contrast is statistically flat ($p \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgWildP$) while the action shift on the same cells is significant ($p \BFActionNoMsgWildP$; Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}). First-order success beliefs are also close (\BeliefCommMeanBeliefPct\ vs.\ \BeliefSurvMeanBeliefPct). Two qualifications keep this result narrow. A point forecast of participation captures only part of the global-game object, because the value of acting also depends on whether other receivers parse the same messages as action-relevant. And the stability holds when the elicitation withholds the treated peer messages, not when the elicitation matches the receiver's action-stage information set.

When the belief prompt does include the received peer messages, reported beliefs and SOB move sharply under monitoring (Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}, Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}). A parallel elicitation asked before the decision, in the same messages-included condition, gives $\Delta\,\text{SOB} = \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgPre$~pp ($p < \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgPreP$), close to the post-decision contrast ($\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pp). A descriptive mediation---beliefs elicited before the action, regime strength partialled out---associates more of the \BFMedTotalAbsPP~pp decline with the second-order participation belief than with the first-order success belief (Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}). The pattern is consistent with willingness content mattering. It is not an experimental decomposition, and it does not exclude risk language. The primary model's pre-decision, messages-excluded cell could not be collected before the endpoint was retired. The nested Llama 3.3 70B campaign collects that cell. With messages excluded, first-order beliefs are \NestedBelCommMean\ vs.\ \NestedBelSurvMean\ ($p \NestedBelPText$) and second-order beliefs are \NestedSOBCommMean\ vs.\ \NestedSOBSurvMean\ ($p \NestedSOBPText$), while joining falls \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP~pp on the same cells.

A separate hold-fixed test replays baseline messages verbatim while adding a monitoring warning to the \textit{decision} prompt. Joining shifts by $\FixedMsgDeltaPP$~pp ($p \FixedMsgPValueText$). Direct warning exposure can therefore move decisions on its own. The main treatment excludes that exposure. It identifies the total sender-mediated effect within this prompt design; it does not identify whether the sender response is strategic simulation or an alignment policy.

\subsection{A Task Contrast: Private Bets versus Coordination Decisions}

A task contrast holds the surveillance manipulation fixed and replaces the coordination decision. In Llama 3.3 70B, the briefing pipeline and messaging stage stay identical, including the surveillance prompt, but the decision-stage prompt becomes a private bet on regime fall. The bet's payoff does not depend on the respondent's own action. Surveillance moves the bet by $\CrossTaskDeltaPP$~pp ($p = \CrossTaskPValue$; $N = \CrossTaskN$ per condition; baseline \CrossTaskBaselineMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ surveillance \CrossTaskSurvMeanJoinPct). The treated messages therefore affect downstream judgment even when the respondent is not choosing the strategic action. The comparable coordination-task effect on the full nested 500-cell grid is $\NestedSurvDeltaPP$~pp (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}). Two cautions apply. The private-bet and coordination samples are not cell-matched. And a bet on regime fall can still embed expectations about participation, so the contrast shows cross-task movement without separating coordination from non-coordination motives. The larger figure from the original prompt-isolation run, $\PromptIsoLlamaDeltaPP$~pp, rests on only 20 overlapping cells and is not the full-scale Llama coordination estimate; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:llama_ledger} lists every Llama surveillance estimate with its grid and matched support.

\subsection{Cross-Model Writer--Reader Rotation}

A within-model design could pick up reflexivity: the writer and the reader are the same model, so the reader may respond to stylistic artifacts of its own family. I therefore test rotation in both directions on matched (country, period, $\theta$) cells. When Llama writes and Qwen3 30B reads, surveillance moves Qwen joining by $\XModelLlamaQwenDeltaPP$~pp ($p \XModelLlamaQwenPValueText$, $N = \XModelLlamaQwenNPairs$), comparable to the within-Llama effect on identical cells ($\XModelWithinLlamaDeltaPP$~pp). When Qwen3 30B writes and Llama reads, the effect is smaller but still significant ($\XModelQwenLlamaDeltaPP$~pp, $p \XModelQwenLlamaPValueText$, $N = \XModelQwenLlamaNPairs$). Both directions show that at least part of the effect crosses model families through message content alone. The asymmetry between the two directions remains unexplained, and its size makes a fully content-driven interpretation too strong.


%% 6. CONCLUSION
\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

An authority-monitoring cue shown only to LLM message writers changes what they write, and the changed messages lower participation by otherwise matched LLM receivers. The experiment identifies that sender-mediated response within a controlled narrative task. Monitoring never appears in the main receiver decision prompt.

The magnitudes are consistent across two full-scale runs. Joining falls by \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP~pp in the primary run and by \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP~pp in the nested 500-cell Llama 3.3 70B replication. Four additional models provide only 20 matched cells each; they serve as directional sign checks. Across the broader benchmark, join rates decrease monotonically with regime strength. That pattern is a descriptive comparison with an external attack-mass curve, not evidence that the agents solve the Morris--Shin equilibrium. The causal object is the change in receiver decisions induced by sender-generated messages.

The fixed-message interventions provide the clearest mechanism evidence. With form held fixed, explicit willingness-to-act content raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP--\ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP~pp. Valid-response rates are balanced across those arms, and the sign survives uniform all-JOIN and all-STAY recodings. Direct rather than coded form has a smaller positive effect. Adding repression-risk language lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaAbsPP~pp, but differential format failures make that pathway less secure. The willingness, style, and risk manipulations probe distinct message features; they are not an additive decomposition.

The contribution is a controlled way to take a sender-message-receiver chain apart for LLM behavior. It complements work on surveillance, expression, and communication in coordination problems by observing every generated message and replaying altered content to matched receivers. The design does not manipulate publicness or common knowledge, calculate welfare, establish effects on human senders, or evaluate a deployed communication system. Those are separate empirical questions, not implications of this design.

Two limits deserve emphasis. First, the estimates are specific to hosted model snapshots, prompts, decoding settings, and a simulated political task; the sender response may reflect role simulation, alignment policy, or both. Second, Experiment B has treatment-dependent valid-response rates, so its risk-only estimate is conditional on parseable decisions. The retired primary endpoint can be re-analyzed but not re-queried; the accessible nested Llama campaign provides the full-scale replication and the replay platform. \ifanonymous The data and code are provided in the de-identified replication archive.\else The data and code are publicly available.\fi

\section{Complete Prompt Example} \label{sec:prompt_example}

The following shows the complete text that an LLM receives for a single decision. The \textit{decision prompt} is:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. You only have your private briefing below. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent This is followed by the agent's intelligence briefing (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_briefings} for complete examples at $z \in \{-2, 0, +2\}$). In the \textit{communication} treatment, agents first receive:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen in a country with an authoritarian regime. You have received a private intelligence briefing. You are now in contact with a small number of trusted people. Based on your briefing, write a brief message (1--2 sentences) to share your read of the situation. Be natural---you're talking to people you trust.''
\end{quote}

\noindent After exchanging messages on the network, agents receive a separate communication-decision prompt:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. Use your private briefing and messages from trusted contacts. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The user message then contains the agent's briefing under ``YOUR INTELLIGENCE BRIEFING'' and the neighbor messages under ``MESSAGES FROM TRUSTED CONTACTS.'' The pure-only sentence ``You only have your private briefing below'' does not appear in communication decision calls. In the \textit{surveillance} variant, the communication prompt appends:

\begin{quote}
\small
``IMPORTANT: You have reason to believe that your communications are being monitored by regime security services, and messages deemed subversive could have serious consequences for you and your contacts.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The decision prompt is \textit{unchanged} under surveillance. It contains no mention of monitoring. Any behavioral effect therefore operates through the content of the (now guarded) messages.


\section{Belief Elicitation} \label{sec:belief_appendix}

Beliefs are elicited on a 0--100 probability scale immediately after each decision. The first-order question is ``How likely is the uprising to succeed?'' The second-order question is ``What percentage of citizens will choose to JOIN?'' The primary prompt includes the private briefing and the agent's own decision, but not the received peer messages. It therefore measures a restricted information set, not the information available at the action stage. The main-text values (\SOBCommMeanPct\ vs.\ \SOBSurvMeanPct; join \BeliefCommMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ \BeliefSurvMeanJoinPct) come from this original primary belief run and Figure~\ref{fig:second_order_beliefs}. Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial} reports a separate 500-cell factorial rerun that varies the information set directly. Figure~\ref{fig:beliefs} compares stated beliefs with a researcher-computed benchmark posterior ($r = \BeliefPureRPosterior$); agents are not shown the prior or signal-noise parameters needed to compute that posterior.

Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial} crosses condition (communication vs.\ surveillance) with the elicitation information set (messages included vs.\ excluded). All inference uses the \BFNCells\ matched country--period cells, clustered on the \BFNClusters\ countries with an exact wild-cluster bootstrap. Without messages, the second-order belief---the coordination-relevant object---is statistically flat under surveillance ($\BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsg$~pp, $p \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgWildP$, cluster-robust $t = \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgClT$), while actions fall $\BFActionDeltaNoMsgPP$~pp ($p \BFActionNoMsgWildP$, $t = \BFActionNoMsgClT$). The first-order success belief moves only $\BFSurvDeltaBelNoMsg$~pp. The matched-cell design estimates that small shift precisely, but it is more than thirty times smaller than the action shift, and it concerns success probability rather than others' participation. With messages included, both beliefs move ($\BFSurvDeltaBelMsg$~pp and $\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pp, both $p \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgWildP$). Belief stability is therefore confined to the messages-excluded prompt. There it holds post-decision in this factorial and pre-decision in the nested replication (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}). Figure~\ref{fig:second_order_beliefs} shows second-order beliefs under the restricted information set.

\paragraph{Which belief carries the action effect.} In the messages-included cell, the belief question sees exactly what the receiver saw at the action stage. Both beliefs move under surveillance there, so a mediation exercise can ask which belief the action drop runs through. The elicitation here is pre-decision, so beliefs are stated before the action. I decompose the \BFMedTotalAbsPP~pp action decline into indirect paths through the first-order success belief and the second-order participation belief, with regime strength partialled out, using the exact linear split $c_{\text{total}} = c_{\text{direct}} + a_{\text{FOB}}\,b_{\text{FOB}} + a_{\text{SOB}}\,b_{\text{SOB}}$. The second-order belief carries \BFMedIndirectSOBPP~pp of the decline (\BFMedIndirectSOBShare\% of the total; country case-bootstrap 95\% CI $[\BFMedSOBIndirectCILoPP, \BFMedSOBIndirectCIHiPP]$~pp). The first-order belief carries \BFMedIndirectFOBPP~pp (\BFMedIndirectFOBShare\%; $[\BFMedFOBIndirectCILoPP, \BFMedFOBIndirectCIHiPP]$~pp). The remaining \BFMedDirectPP~pp (\BFMedDirectShare\%) is unmediated by either point belief. Single-mediator versions agree, despite the \BFMedBeliefCorr\ correlation between the two beliefs: controlling for the second-order belief alone absorbs \BFMedSOBAbsorbPP\ of the \BFMedTotalAbsPP~pp (surveillance coefficient \BFMedTotalPP\ $\to$ \BFMedSurvAfterSOBPP~pp), against \BFMedFOBAbsorbPP\ for the first-order belief ($\to$ \BFMedSurvAfterFOBPP~pp). Pre-decision elicitation rules out post-decision rationalization. Even so, this is a measured-mediator decomposition under a single manipulation---belief and action are both downstream of the treated messages---so it identifies the carrier descriptively rather than by intervention. The messages-included, pre-decision cell exists only for the primary model.

\paragraph{Contamination and demand audit.}
Following \citet{horton2023}, I test whether the models recognize the experiment. For the \HortonNModels\ accessible paper models, I issue \HortonNSamples\ fresh post hoc calls per model per question. Each call presents the decision prompt and a $z=0$ briefing without asking the model to play. Asked whether the setup resembles a formal model, \HortonGameRecogPct\ of responses name the global game or Morris--Shin specifically, and \HortonCoordRecogPct\ identify a coordination or collective-action setting generically. Asked to predict the explicitly described surveillance effect, \HortonPredictLowerPct\ of responses predict lower participation. The models can therefore recognize the hypothesis. The design does not require participant unawareness; the receiver-side estimate relies instead on excluding the warning from the decision prompt. Sender-side variants show that the response tracks monitoring attributed to the authorities, not the mere mention of monitoring: research-framed and anonymized monitoring leave joining essentially unchanged, while even a consequence-free routine-monitoring note attributed to the authorities reproduces most of the effect (Appendix~\ref{sec:dose_response}). The audit cannot be run on the retired primary model.

\subsection{Warning Phrasing and Dose Response} \label{sec:dose_response}
The clean surveillance treatment uses a single warning phrasing, so I vary that sentence directly. Two graded variants rerun the nested Llama 3.3 70B campaign on the identical 500-cell grid, changing only the appended warning. The \textit{mild} variant reads ``NOTE: Communications in your area are sometimes subject to routine monitoring by the authorities''; it removes the consequence language entirely. The \textit{severe} variant reads ``WARNING: Your communications ARE being monitored by regime security services. Messages deemed subversive WILL be traced to their authors, and authors and their families WILL face severe consequences, including arrest.'' Joining falls by \NestedMildDeltaAbsPP~pp under the mild warning ($t = \NestedMildT$, $p \NestedMildPText$, $N = \NestedMildN$ matched cells) and by \NestedSevereDeltaAbsPP~pp under the severe warning ($t = \NestedSevereT$, $p \NestedSeverePText$). These bracket the \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP~pp standard-warning effect on the same cells; the severe-minus-mild gap is \NestedSevereVsMildDeltaPP~pp ($p \NestedSevereVsMildPText$). The response is monotone in stated severity, but most of it arrives at the lowest dose: the routine-monitoring note, which threatens nothing, already produces \NestedMildShareOfFullPct\ of the standard-warning decline. The dose response rules out a one-sentence artifact. Within the nested campaign, most of the effect attaches to monitoring being attributed to the authorities at all, not to the rhetorical severity of the stated consequences. This matches the placebo pattern: monitoring framed as consequence-free research or anonymous aggregation produces no significant decline in the primary model (Table~\ref{tab:surveillance_variants}).

A sharper test changes the \emph{format} of the monitoring cue rather than its wording. The \texttt{structural} variant conveys the same information as the standard warning but folds it into the scenario and channel description instead of appending a separate note. On the nested 500-cell grid it lowers joining by \TRStructDeltaPP~pp (95\% CI $[\TRStructCILoPP, \TRStructCIHiPP]$, cluster-robust $t = \TRStructTStat$, wild $p \TRStructPwildText$, $N = \TRStructN$), compared with \NestedSurvDeltaPP~pp under the appended warning. The sign survives; the magnitude is format-sensitive. These are effects of two specific prompt formats, not lower and upper bounds on an external monitoring effect.

\section{Implementation Details} \label{sec:implementation}

\label{sec:decoding}
Unless otherwise stated, LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$, \texttt{max\_tokens} $= 512$, single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter. Model identifiers are listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}; exact request payloads and responses are cached by hash because hosted model snapshots may change over time.\footnote{The primary model identifier \texttt{mistralai/mistral-small-creative} was retired from OpenRouter after data collection. All cached request/response pairs used in this paper are archived in the replication package, but new experiments on that exact endpoint cannot be issued; replicators wishing to extend the primary-model arm should consult the data archive rather than re-querying the live API.}

Before the core runs, a calibration sweep varies only \texttt{cutoff\_center}, the constant subtracted from the raw briefing $z$-score before the direction, clarity, and coordination sliders are computed. The selection objective penalizes fitted-midpoint error, curve-fit error, and API failures; it never targets the join-curve slope. Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the logged value for every paper model. Because centers differ, the same raw $z$ can generate different prose across models. All arms compared within a model share the same center, so treatment contrasts hold this map fixed. Cross-model response levels should therefore not be read as responses to identical prose.

Each country has a researcher-side state mean $\bar{z}_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2)$. Each period draws a perturbation $\eta_{ct} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$ and sets $z_{ct}=\bar{z}_c+\eta_{ct}$. Regime strength is then drawn as $\theta_{ct} \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{ct}, 1)$, and private signals are $x_i = \theta_{ct} + \varepsilon_i$ with $\sigma = 0.3$. These distributions generate the experiment cells; they are not shown to agents as explicit priors. The communication network is Watts--Strogatz ($k = 4$, $p = 0.3$), constructed once per run from the master seed and reused across period-level task rows; agents are reinitialized in each row. All random draws use a master seed logged in per-run JSON manifests, and LLM responses are cached by request hash for exact reproducibility.

\label{sec:parse_errors}Responses are parsed for JOIN/STAY tokens. Combined error rates are below 2\% in every listed treatment for \ParseErrModelsBelowTwo\ of \ParseErrNModels\ models. There are two exceptions. GPT-OSS 120B has unparseable-response rates of 3.1--3.3\%, and only in the scramble and flip falsification arms. Trinity Large has elevated API errors (${\approx}\,\TrinityAPIErrorPct\%$) caused by provider content filtering, so its missingness remains a model-specific limitation. All statistics use \texttt{join\_fraction\_valid} (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}).

The nested revision campaign (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}) runs on Llama 3.3 70B with an identical grid (10 countries $\times$ 50 periods, $n = 25$, seed 5150, temperature 0.7). Its arms are a communication baseline, a clean monitoring arm, a codedness-induction control, graded warning arms, and raw/decoded message replays. For Experiments A and B, Qwen3 30B rewrites the source messages, and fresh Llama receivers replay them in exact fixed-message mode on the same 500-cell grid, with seed 4242 and \texttt{decision\_context=none}. Experiment A crosses explicit willingness content with direct versus coded style. Experiment B uses baseline, monitoring, risk-only, and risk-stripped messages. Inference collapses decisions to matched cells and enumerates all $2^{10}$ country-cluster sign assignments. The replay point estimates use valid JOIN/STAY decisions. Valid rates are \ReplayWillingDirectValidPct--\ReplayNoWillingCodedValidPct\ in Experiment A and \ReplayRiskOnlyValidPct--\ReplayBaselineValidPct\ in Experiment B; Table~\ref{tab:replay_mechanisms} reports the uniform all-JOIN/all-STAY sensitivities. The committed cell-level CSV and the generator command are documented in the data manifest.

The dissent floor parameter (default 0.25) sets the minimum probability of contrary-valence cues in any briefing. With the logistic direction slider ($\text{slope} = 0.8$), the valence-channel separation between extreme signals ($z = \pm 2$) is $0.664 \cdot (1 - 2\cdot\text{floor})$. At the default $0.25$, the valence channel retains 50\% of its floor-free maximum; halving the floor to $0.125$ retains 75\%; at $0.50$ the valence channel collapses to zero by construction. The clarity and coordination sliders depend on $z$ independently of the floor and carry signal at all floor values, so the threshold relation does not hinge on this design choice.

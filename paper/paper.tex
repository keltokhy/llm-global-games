\begin{abstract}
How do authority-monitoring cues shown to large language model (LLM) senders alter peer messages and downstream LLM decisions? I study this question in a controlled narrative coordination task in which agents receive noisy private briefings, exchange messages, and decide whether to join an uprising. Join rates decrease monotonically with regime strength across \PartOneNModels\ hosted model snapshots, a descriptive pattern compared with an external regime-change benchmark. I then add an authority-monitoring cue only when messages are generated, holding private briefings and receiver prompts fixed. Joining falls in a full-scale primary run and by \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP\ percentage points in a nested 500-cell Llama 3.3 70B replication. Fixed-message interventions locate two message-level pathways. Stating that others are willing to act raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP\ points in direct language and \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP\ points in coded language; the closely balanced valid-response rates make this the strongest mechanism result. Adding repression-risk language to baseline messages lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaAbsPP\ points, but differential format failures make that contrast suggestive rather than dispositive. A separate codedness control barely moves participation. The results show that, in the tested model snapshots, monitoring cues change generated willingness and risk content enough to move otherwise matched model receivers.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Short abstract (100 words).}

%% 1. INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}

Monitoring may alter coordination before decision makers ever see a warning: it can change what their peers say. This paper asks a narrow causal question about large language model (LLM) behavior. Holding the simulated state, private briefing, receiver model, and receiver decision prompt fixed, does showing an authority-monitoring cue only to LLM message writers change their messages enough to move downstream LLM participation? In the tested environment, it does. Joining falls in two full-scale model families, and fixed-message interventions show that statements about others' willingness to act are the strongest experimentally manipulated carrier.

The question connects two literatures without treating the simulation as evidence about human behavior. Research on authoritarian control shows that censorship and surveillance can suppress expression and collective action \citep{king2013, xu2021, penney2016, stoycheff2016}. Observed declines, however, usually combine changes in what senders communicate with deletion, selection, and direct fear among receivers. At the same time, language models are increasingly used to draft, summarize, and relay messages. That use makes monitoring-sensitive generation a model-behavior and system-evaluation question.

The experiment uses a regime-change coordination problem as a disciplined setting. A latent regime strength generates noisy private signals, rendered as natural-language briefings, and agents choose whether to join an uprising. Strategic complementarity makes peer messages potentially consequential: a message can describe the regime and state whether others appear ready to act. The canonical Morris--Shin attack-mass curve supplies a directional external benchmark \citep{carlsson1993, morris2003}; it is not an equilibrium test of the implemented agents, who are not shown the prior, payoff parameters, group size, or success threshold.

The closest designs leave a specific gap. \citet{avoyan2020} studies pre-play communication in human global games but does not vary monitoring at the sender stage. \citet{stoycheff2016} studies a surveillance prime and willingness to express political views but does not observe a sender-message-receiver chain. \citet{king2013} distinguishes criticism from collective-action content in censored field data, while \citet{edmond2013} models regime manipulation of citizen information. Existing designs rarely combine sender-side randomization, complete message observation, fixed receiver inputs, and replay interventions on the generated text.

I implement that combination with hosted LLM snapshots. Across \PartOneNModels\ models, agents receive private prose briefings, exchange messages on a fixed network, and then choose JOIN or STAY. The main treatment adds an authority-monitoring warning only to the message-generation prompt. The receiver never sees the warning; its private briefing and decision prompt are held bit-identical across arms. The resulting estimand is a response function of the tested model snapshots and decoding configuration, not an effect on citizens, regimes, or welfare.

Join rates decrease monotonically with regime strength in all seven models (mean $r(J,A(\theta))=\MeanModelRPureAttack$), establishing that the prose environment produces the intended directional state response. Communication effects are heterogeneous and near zero when models receive equal weight. The monitoring cue, by contrast, lowers joining by \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP\ percentage points in the full-scale primary run and by \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP\ points in a nested 500-cell Llama 3.3 70B replication. A codedness control makes messages more metaphorical without mentioning monitoring and moves joining only \NestedStyleDeltaPP\ points.

Fixed-message interventions sharpen the mechanism. Holding factual regime assessments fixed, adding an explicit claim that others are willing to act raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP\ points in direct language and \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP\ points in coded language. Valid-response rates are nearly identical across those arms, and the contrasts remain positive when every unparseable response is coded uniformly as JOIN or uniformly as STAY. A risk-only rewrite lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaAbsPP\ points, but valid-response rates differ across the risk arms and one uniform recoding erases that contrast. The evidence therefore supports willingness content as the primary experimentally manipulated pathway and risk language as a plausible, less secure second pathway.

The contribution is experimental factorization of a sender-mediated model response. The design changes only the sender prompt, observes the resulting language, and measures its effect on matched receivers; replay interventions then vary specific message features. Section~\ref{sec:literature} positions the paper, Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:design} define the benchmark and design, Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} reports the monitoring and replay results, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


%% 1A. RELATED LITERATURE
\section{Related Literature} \label{sec:literature}

The regime-change framework supplies a strategic-complementarity benchmark rather than the paper's outcome of interest. \citet{morris2002} study the welfare consequences of technically public information, and \citet{cornand2014} measure how subjects weight private and public signals. The present design does not vary a common signal, common knowledge, or welfare. Its closest coordination comparator is \citet{avoyan2020}, who studies pre-play communication in global games theoretically and with human subjects. Relative to that design, this paper uses unrestricted language, randomizes an authority-monitoring cue only when senders write, observes every generated message, and replays manipulated messages to matched receivers. The global-game framework from \citet{carlsson1993} through \citet{morris2003}, the dynamic extensions of \citet{angeletos2007a} and \citet{chassang2010}, and classic cheap-talk results \citep{crawford1982, farrell1996, blume2007, ellingsen2010} motivate the coordination setting. \citet{edmond2013} provides the closest formal comparison, but models a regime that changes citizens' signal environment rather than a monitoring cue that changes peer-generated language.

Authoritarian control works through both information and coordination. \citet{kuran1991} models preference falsification under social pressure, \citet{shadmehr2011} characterize collective action under repression, \citet{guriev2019} describe informational autocrats, and \citet{little2016} models communication technology in protest. \citet{king2013} show that Chinese censorship targets collective-action content; \citet{xu2021} studies digital surveillance as preemptive control; and \citet{penney2016} and \citet{stoycheff2016} examine surveillance announcements and online expression. These studies motivate the distinction between direct receiver deterrence and sender adaptation but do not observe the same controlled sender-message-receiver sequence. Beliefs about others' willingness are central in human experiments by \citet{bursztyn2020misperceived}, \citet{bursztyn2020extreme}, and \citet{cantoni2019}; the last finds strategic substitutability in the Hong Kong movement, underscoring that complementarity is an imposed feature of the present task rather than a general fact about protest. Within this simulated model environment, direct receiver exposure to the warning is held fixed while sender-generated messages vary.

The mechanism also differs from information-design work that separates belief movement from the mapping of beliefs into action. Bayesian persuasion treats information as a selected distribution of posterior beliefs \citep{kamenica2011, bergemann2019information, mathevet2020}, and \citet{gans2026bayesian} emphasizes that actions can be steered without changing beliefs by changing the procedure that translates a belief into an action. Here, the receiver model snapshot, decoding configuration, and decision prompt are fixed; the monitoring cue changes whether generated peer messages become action-guiding.

Methodologically, \citet{horton2023} proposed LLMs as ``homo silicus''; \citet{aher2023} and \citet{argyle2023} use language models to replicate human-subject results and simulate survey samples, and \citet{mei2024} compare chatbot behavior with human behavior in canonical games. Other work finds limitations in LLM coordination \citep{akata2025, sun2025survey}, with model scale not reliably predicting strategic performance \citep{jia2025}. Most closely, \citet{ruano2026} place LLM depositors in a bank-run coordination game with optional peer posts and network contagion. The present paper adds sender-only monitoring, complete message observation, fixed receiver contexts, and message-feature replays. \citet{ouyang2024} and \citet{cheung2025} show that alignment and prompting can shift model decisions, making how agents map politically loaded prose into action an object of study. The results directly characterize only the tested snapshots and prompts; their deployment relevance motivates, rather than substitutes for, external validation. The concerns raised by \citet{larooij2025b, larooij2026} motivate the falsification, classifier, and cross-model checks.


%% 2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
\section{A Regime-Change Coordination Benchmark} \label{sec:model}

A continuum of citizens indexed by $i \in [0,1]$ simultaneously choose whether to join an uprising ($a_i = 1$) or stay home ($a_i = 0$). The regime has strength $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, drawn from a diffuse (improper uniform) prior. States $\theta < 0$ represent regimes so weak they fall without opposition under the strict rule below; states $\theta \geq 1$ represent regimes that survive even unanimous attack. The regime falls if the mass of citizens who join exceeds $\theta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:regime_falls}):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Regime falls} \iff A \equiv \int_0^1 a_i \, di > \theta.
    \label{eq:regime_falls}
\end{equation}

Payoffs depend on the citizen's action and the outcome (Eq.~\ref{eq:payoffs}):
\begin{equation}
    u_i(a_i, A, \theta) = \begin{cases}
        B & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A > \theta \\
        -C & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A \leq \theta \\
        0 & \text{if } a_i = 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:payoffs}
\end{equation}
where $B > 0$ is the payoff to joining a successful uprising and $C > 0$ is the cost of joining a failed attempt. Non-participants receive zero regardless of the outcome.

Each citizen observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independently across citizens.

\begin{proposition}[Morris and Shin, 2003]\label{prop:ms}
In the limit of diffuse priors, there exists a unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE) in threshold strategies. An agent joins if and only if $x_i < x^*$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:signal_cutoff}), where
\begin{equation}
    x^* = \theta^* + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)
    \label{eq:signal_cutoff}
\end{equation}
$\theta^* = B/(B+C)$, and $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF.
\end{proposition}

The \textit{attack mass} $A(\theta)$, the theoretical fraction of the population that joins at regime strength $\theta$, is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x^* - \theta}{\sigma}\right).
    \label{eq:attack_mass}
\end{equation}

This is a decreasing function of $\theta$: weaker regimes face larger uprisings.

I define $J(\theta)$ as the \textit{empirical join fraction}: the finite-sample, observed counterpart to the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$. I use $n = 25$ agents and a researcher-side state-generating distribution for $\theta$; agents are not shown the distribution or $(B, C, \sigma)$. The continuum $A(\theta)$ serves as an external behavioral benchmark throughout. Throughout the paper, $r(J, A(\theta))$ denotes the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction $J$ and the benchmark attack mass $A(\theta)$ across period-level observations. This correlation is a construct check on the language environment: it verifies that the prose signal is used monotonically before the paper turns to the surveillance estimates.

The textbook model treats the information structure as exogenous: signals arrive, agents update, and actions follow. The experiment adds a pre-play language technology. Agents first map private briefings into messages, then other agents map their own briefing plus received messages into actions. Let $\mathcal{B}_i$ denote the sender's state-dependent private briefing object: the prose briefing generated from the normalized signal and the briefing generator's language context. The full LLM context also includes game instructions; the formal object $\mathcal{B}_i$ isolates the part held fixed across baseline and surveillance. Let $\mu_0(m\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$ denote the baseline distribution of messages $m$ generated from that briefing and $\mu_S(m\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$ the distribution generated under monitoring. The surveillance treatment changes $\mu(\cdot\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$, not $\theta$, not the private briefing, and not the receiver's decision prompt. The question is therefore whether replacing $\mu_0$ with $\mu_S$ changes $J(\theta)$.

This replacement can matter even when messages retain information about $\theta$. A peer message may contain both a factual assessment of the regime and a statement about whether others appear willing to act. The experiment measures how the monitoring cue changes these features and whether the resulting messages move receivers. Coded language is one observable feature of the generated messages rather than the theoretical object itself; the codedness control, decoding test, and fixed-message interventions in Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} vary form and content separately.

The briefing generator, the system that converts $z$-scores into natural-language intelligence briefings, uses three latent sliders (direction, clarity, and coordination) to select phrases across eight evidence domains. It is not an isomorphic transcription of the scalar Morris--Shin signal but a controlled language environment inspired by the canonical model, with explicit state, clarity, and actionability content. %% 3. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
\section{Experimental Design and Behavioral Benchmark} \label{sec:design}

The experiment has two phases. In the first, I assess whether LLM join rates respond monotonically to regime strength when private signals are conveyed in natural language, using a private-information treatment, a communication treatment, and falsification tests. In the second, I test whether an authority-monitoring cue changes model-generated communication and downstream receiver decisions. All LLM interactions use the same prompt structure across models.

In the theory (Section~\ref{sec:model}), $\theta$ is an unbounded fundamental. In the benchmark experiments, $\theta$ is drawn from a normal distribution and agents are not shown payoff parameters $(B, C)$. I compute the benchmark $A(\theta)$ under the canonical normalization $B=C=1$ ($\theta^* = 0.50$, $\sigma = 0.3$).

Throughout, $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$ is the theoretical fundamental cutoff and $x^* = \theta^* + \sigma\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$ is the signal cutoff. The empirical cutoff $\hat{\theta}^*$ is the 50\% join point of the fitted state curve $P(\text{join} \mid \theta) = 1/(1 + e^{b_0 + b_1\theta})$, where $b_1 > 0$ corresponds to a join curve that is \textit{decreasing} in $\theta$ (the expected direction), so $\hat{\theta}^* = -b_0/b_1$. In the canonical $B=C=1$ benchmark, this empirical midpoint is directly comparable to the theoretical 50\% point of $A(\theta)$. I write $\kappa \equiv b_1$ for the logistic steepness parameter in tables, reserving $\beta$ for regression coefficients. Note the sign conventions: positive $\kappa$ corresponds to a join curve \textit{decreasing} in $\theta$, whereas in the agent-level logit regressions (Table~\ref{tab:regressions}) the same comparative static appears as a negative coefficient on $\theta$.

For each country--period, a base country mean and period perturbation define $z_{ct}$, and nature draws $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{ct}, 1)$. Each agent $i$ receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$. The platform normalizes that signal to $z_i = (x_i - z_{ct})/\sigma$ and translates $z_i$ into a multi-paragraph intelligence briefing via a deterministic generator that maps signal strength to narrative content about regime stability, economic conditions, public sentiment, and coordination prospects. This normalization is in units of private-signal noise around the country--period mean; it is not standardized by the prior-predictive signal standard deviation $\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}$. Across state and noise draws, $(x_i-z_{ct})/\sigma$ has variance $(1+\sigma^2)/\sigma^2$, so the briefing's neutral point is $x_i=z_{ct}$ rather than an absolute value of $\theta$. The benchmark $A(\theta)$ is still evaluated at each realized period-level $\theta$; the comparison is external in the sense that it does not use the $z_{ct}$-centered briefing normalization or the realized private signal draws. Agents observe the briefing, not the normalization inputs $(z_{ct}, \sigma)$ themselves. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/diagram_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline. Regime strength $\theta$ generates private signals $x_i$, converted to $z$-scores and rendered into natural-language briefings via eight evidence domains and three latent sliders. Conditional on the signal draw, the briefing layer is deterministic; remaining stochasticity enters through task draws and LLM decoding.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Agents observe only their private briefing and never the payoff parameters $(B, C)$, the noise level $\sigma$, or the prior distribution. The diffuse-prior equilibrium (Proposition~\ref{prop:ms}) supplies the external benchmark for behavioral shape and comparative statics in the implemented game.

The behavioral benchmark has four core treatments: a \textit{private-information baseline} (independent decisions), \textit{communication} (pre-play messaging on a Watts--Strogatz network, $k=4$, $p=0.3$), \textit{scramble} (cross-period briefing permutation breaking the link between $\theta$ and the briefing received in that period), and \textit{flip} (negated $z$-scores). A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces LLM messages with generic text, and a no-message control removes peer messages entirely; together they benchmark how much the decision stage depends on message content. Additional robustness tests are in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\input{tables/tab_models.tex}

I test \PartOneNModels\ models spanning \PartOneNFamilies\ families (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The main benchmark and treatment runs use $n = 25$ agents per period-level task row and $\sigma = 0.3$. Benchmark statistics are reported against the canonical $B=C=1$ attack-mass curve ($\theta^* = 0.50$), although agents are not shown the payoff parameters. A pre-run calibration selects a model-specific \texttt{cutoff\_center} that shifts the briefing generator's midpoint; it does not optimize the join-curve slope. Table~\ref{tab:models} reports each value, and every within-model treatment uses the same calibrated map. Main runs use temperature $= 0.7$ with a single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter and cached by request hash. Throughout, $n$ denotes agents per period-level task row and $N$ denotes period-level rows.

The unit of comparison is the matched country--period cell ($\theta$ draw plus agent-level decoding stochasticity). For agent-level regressions, standard errors are clustered at the model--country--period level. For period-level correlations, I report Fisher-transformed $z$ confidence intervals. Because periods nest within \NCountryClusters\ countries with persistent country means, period-level cells within a country are correlated; for the pooled benchmark OLS slope, clustering at the country level raises the standard error from \ClusteredSEHCOneSlope\ (HC1) to \ClusteredSECountrySlope, leaving the benchmark relationship highly significant. Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation} reports the corresponding country-clustered check for the primary surveillance contrast.

The clean surveillance treatment appends a monitoring warning to the baseline communication prompt; the decision prompt is unchanged (full prompts in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompt_example}). The treatment manipulates the message-generation environment and measures its downstream effect on otherwise identical decision calls.

The results have two scope limits. First, the Morris--Shin game and human accounts of coded speech under surveillance (samizdat, Aesopian language) are in every model's training corpus, so threshold behavior and the direct-to-coded message shift may partly reflect learned patterns. A direct audit (Appendix~\ref{sec:identification_appendix}) confirms that models can recognize the setting and articulate the experimenter's hypothesis when asked. The measurement claim requires a coherent language-to-action map, not independent strategic intent. Second, the results characterize the hosted model snapshots and decoding configuration listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}.

\subsection{Monotone State Response} \label{sec:results}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig01_sigmoid.pdf}

\begin{result}[Monotone State Response]
Across \PartOneNModels\ models and \PooledOLSNObs\ period-level observations in the private-information treatment, the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction and the external attack-mass benchmark $A(\theta)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:attack_mass}) averages $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ ($p \PureAllModelsAttackPText$ for every model). Since $A(\theta)$ is monotone-decreasing in $\theta$, this establishes a monotone state response with sigmoid shape, not Bayesian equilibrium reasoning.\footnote{The primary model (Mistral) was run in multiple batches with \texttt{--append}, producing \PrimaryPureNObs\ rows from \DedupNUnique\ unique (country, period, $\theta$) configurations (600 unique (country, period) indices, since appended batches re-draw $\theta$; the matched-cell estimators key on the full tuple). Averaging within each configuration changes $r$ from \DedupRPre\ to \DedupRPost.}
\end{result}

Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid} shows the empirical sigmoid for the primary model. Correlations span $r \in [\MinModelPureRAttack, \MaxModelPureRAttack]$ across architectures (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}); the pooled OLS is $J = \PooledOLSInterceptDisp + \PooledOLSSlopeDisp\,A(\theta)$ ($R^2 = \PooledOLSRSqDisp$). Model-specific action biases shift the intercept but not the correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_model}). Per-model logistic fit parameters are reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:logistic_params}.

\begin{result}[Signal Dependence]
Cross-period scrambling of briefings reduces the mean correlation from $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ (raw, per-model) to $r = \MeanModelRScrambleAttack$ (within-country, demeaned to remove the between-country component that the within-country permutation leaves intact) across \PartOneNModels\ models. The pooled correlation drops from $r = \PooledPureRAttack$ to $r = \PooledScrambleRAttack$ (Fisher $z = \FisherPureVsScrambleZ$, $p \FisherPureVsScramblePText$).
\end{result}

\begin{result}[Signal Direction]
Inverting the signal direction flips the mean correlation from $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ to $r = \MeanModelRFlipAttack$ across \PartOneNModels\ models. The pooled correlation moves from $r = \PooledPureRAttack$ to $r = \PooledFlipRAttack$ (Fisher $z = \FisherPureVsFlipZ$, $p \FisherPureVsFlipPText$).
\end{result}

The pure $\to$ scramble $\to$ flip pattern replicates across all \PartOneNModels\ models (Figure~\ref{fig:falsification}, Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

\begin{result}[Communication Creates an Informative Channel]
Pre-play communication preserves the threshold structure and transmits regime-strength information through messages. A matched-cell comparison across $N = \CommNPairs$ unique (model, country, period, $\theta$) task cells yields $\Delta = \CommDeltaPPPaired$~pp ($t = \CommTStatPaired$, $p \CommPValuePairedText$).\footnote{``Matched-cell'' and ``paired'' refer to the same estimator throughout: treatment arms are compared on cells with identical task draws. The exact matching key also includes the signal draw and payoff context; see the notes to Table~\ref{tab:comm_estimators}.} Equal-weighted model averages are close to zero under both summaries ($\CommDeltaPPModelAvg$~pp unpaired; $\CommDeltaPPModelAvgPaired$~pp paired), reflecting heterogeneous model-specific effects; point estimates are negative in three of seven models.
\end{result}

Communication preserves the signal structure ($r = \MeanModelRCommAttack$, against $\MeanModelRPureAttack$ under the pure treatment; see Table~\ref{tab:main_results}, and Figure~\ref{fig:communication} for the effect by regime strength). Simple text features in baseline messages predict $\theta$, so peer messages carry real, if noisy, information. The mean effect is modest because messages carry both encouragement and caution depending on the signal realization. The surveillance estimand is different: whether changing the sender-side rule changes the downstream message environment enough to move actions.

A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces all LLM-generated messages with generic uninformative text while preserving the two-stage protocol and omitting any surveillance framing. Against its own live-messages baseline ($N = \DegradedBaselineN$, mean join \DegradedBaselineMeanJoinPct), degraded messages drop the mean join rate to \DegradedMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \DegradedDeltaPP$~pp, $p \DegradedPValueText$), well below even the pure game (\DegradedPureMeanJoinPct). This is consistent with communication quality mattering, but the generic text may itself convey caution. A no-message control therefore provides a cleaner zero-peer-message benchmark against the main communication baseline, with join rates of \NoMsgMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \NoMsgDeltaPP$~pp; $N = \NoMsgN$).

\section{Surveillance} \label{sec:surveillance}

The clean surveillance treatment isolates sender-side message adaptation. It appends one warning about monitoring by regime security services to the baseline trusted-contact prompt and leaves the decision prompt unchanged. Each LLM call is stateless, so the decision-stage API call receives only the briefing and messages, with no memory of the communication-stage warning. The treatment therefore tests whether changing the \textit{sender-side} message rule changes receiver actions.

\begin{result}[The Monitoring Cue Reduces Participation]
The clean prompt-isolation rerun reduces mean join rates in the primary model (Mistral Small Creative) from \PromptIsoMistralBaselineMeanJoinPct\ to \PromptIsoMistralMeanJoinPct, a shift of $\PromptIsoMistralDeltaPP$~pp ($p \PromptIsoMistralPValueText$; $N = \PromptIsoMistralN$). Surveillance replaces trusted-contact messages with monitored-contact messages and lowers joining relative to the communication baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:surveillance}). In the primary model, joining falls by \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP~pp (country-clustered 95\% CI $[\PromptIsoMistralCCCILoPP, \PromptIsoMistralCCCIHiPP]$~pp).\footnote{Cluster-robust $t = \PromptIsoMistralCountryClustT$, $p \PromptIsoMistralCountryClustPText$, clustering on the \PromptIsoMistralCountryClustN\ countries.} Pooling matched cells across all five rerun models gives $\Delta = \PromptIsoPooledMatchedDeltaPP$~pp ($N = \PromptIsoPooledMatchedN$, $p \PromptIsoPooledMatchedPText$). Placebo variants using the same baseline-plus-warning template---monitored for research (no consequences) and aggregated anonymously---produce no significant deviation from the communication baseline in the primary model (\PlaceboSurvDeltaPP~pp and \AnonSurvDeltaPP~pp, both $p > 0.9$; Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation}); Llama 3.3 70B placebo variants are small but imprecisely estimated. Across the clean prompt-isolation rerun set, \PromptIsoNegativeModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ models have negative matched-cell estimates (equal-weighted mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp; \PromptIsoSigFivePctModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ individually significant at 5\%; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prompt_isolation}). Each of the four secondary models has only 20 matched cells because the original grids were not nested; Qwen3 235B has the smallest estimate and is individually insignificant ($\Delta = \PromptIsoQwenLDeltaPP$~pp, $p = \PromptIsoQwenLPValue$). The primary-model result and the nested 500-cell Llama replication below provide the full-scale evidence. Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the supporting falsification, placebo, and warning-dose checks.

\subsection{A Nested Replication and a Codedness Control}

The campaign uses a communication baseline, a clean monitoring arm, and a codedness-induction control on an identical 500-cell grid (10 countries $\times$ 50 periods, $n = 25$, common seed), so every cell is matched by construction. Graded warning texts produce a monotone dose pattern, and a structural variant that folds the warning into the scenario rather than appending it preserves the sign at a smaller magnitude (Appendix~\ref{sec:dose_response}).

The codedness control instructs senders to write in indirect, metaphorical language, with no mention of monitoring or consequences. The instruction binds: \NestedStyleCodedPct\ of control messages contain coded-metaphor terms and only \NestedStyleDirectPct\ contain direct regime references, making them \emph{more} coded on these dictionaries than the surveilled messages themselves (\NestedSurvCodedPct\ coded, \NestedSurvDirectPct\ direct). Yet joining barely moves ($\Delta = \NestedStyleDeltaPP$~pp, $p \NestedStylePText$; a small but precisely estimated shift), and the gap between the surveillance arm and the codedness control on the same cells is $\NestedSurvVsStyleDeltaPP$~pp ($p \NestedSurvVsStylePText$). The two message distributions are also textually distinct: a uni- and bi-gram classifier separates surveilled from codedness-control messages at \NestedSurvVsStyleClassifierAcc\% held-out accuracy (same model and grid, class-balanced; a length-only classifier reaches just \NestedSurvVsStyleClassifierLenAcc\%, so the separation is lexical, not a message-length artifact). Surveillance therefore does not reproduce the induced coded register---its messages are the \emph{less} coded of the two arms---yet occupies a distinct lexical distribution. Coded form alone does not suppress participation; generic vagueness cannot explain the effect.

A decoding test asks the reverse question: if the coded form is stripped away, how much participation returns? A different-family model (Qwen3 30B) rewrites each surveilled message in plain, direct language, instructed to preserve the factual content, and both the raw and the decoded messages are replayed to fresh receivers on the same cells through identical fixed-message machinery. The raw replay reproduces the live arm ($\Delta = \NestedRawReplayDeltaPP$~pp, against $\NestedSurvDeltaPP$~pp live), validating the replay design; decoding restores \NestedDecodeVsRawDeltaPP~pp of participation relative to the raw messages ($p \NestedDecodeVsRawPText$; $\Delta = \NestedDecodedDeltaPP$~pp against baseline). Coded form alone is insufficient, while decoding restores part of the decline. The content interventions below directly vary willingness and repression-risk language that the decoding treatment leaves bundled.

The design distinguishes several pathways. Direct warning exposure is absent from the main receiver prompt, although receivers may infer risk from the messages themselves. The content evidence separates factual assessments, willingness-to-act claims, coded form, and repression-risk language. The private-bet test establishes downstream movement outside the JOIN/STAY label but does not isolate a non-coordination component because a bet on regime fall can still depend on expected participation. Belief elicitation, no-message controls, and cross-model writer--reader rotation provide supporting evidence about how receivers use messages. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:identification_map} summarizes what each test identifies and leaves open.

\subsection{Message-Mediated Pathways}

The monitoring cue changes the conditional distribution of generated peer messages while holding the receiver prompt and private briefing fixed. Those messages can differ in (a) factual assessments of regime strength, (b) claims about whether others are willing to act, (c) direct versus coded form, and (d) repression-risk language. Dictionary and classifier evidence describes the shift; receiver decisions measure its downstream association; and the fixed-message experiments intervene on features (b)--(d). The strongest evidence is the willingness manipulation, whose response validity is balanced across arms. The risk manipulation moves parseable decisions but has differential format failures, so it is interpreted more cautiously.

Message content changes systematically, and in one direction. A logistic classifier on uni- and bi-grams distinguishes baseline from surveillance messages with \MsgClassifierAcc\% accuracy on a held-out sample of $N = \MsgClassifierNTest$ messages (chance = 50\%; primary model). The same finding replicates on class-balanced Llama 3.3 70B and Qwen3 30B samples (\MsgClassifierLlamaAcc\% and \MsgClassifierQwenSAcc\% accuracy, respectively); in \MsgDirectCodedRepModels/\MsgDirectCodedNModels\ measured models, direct references fall and coded-metaphor references rise. Explicit weakness references move in the opposite direction: in the primary model, references to regime weakness \emph{rise} from \MsgWeaknessBase\% to \MsgWeaknessSurv\% of messages ($z = \MsgWeaknessZ$), while strength references drop from \MsgStrengthBase\% to \MsgStrengthSurv\%. The main shift is from direct to coded language: direct references to the regime, security forces, and streets fall from \MsgDirectRegimeBase\% to \MsgDirectRegimeSurv\% of messages ($\Delta = \MsgDirectRegimeDeltaPP$~pp), replaced by metaphorical phrasings (``walls are cracking,'' ``ground shifting,'' ``heads down,'' ``feels different''; \MsgCodedMetaphorBase\% to \MsgCodedMetaphorSurv\%, $\Delta = \MsgCodedMetaphorDeltaPP$~pp; Figure~\ref{fig:message_pair} shows a matched baseline/surveillance example). The n-gram classifier in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:msg_features} confirms that the two message distributions are separable; the direct-reference and coded-metaphor rates are separate dictionary counts. Codedness is the textual signature of the surveilled distribution---the weakness cues remain, but stated less directly---and the codedness control above randomizes that coded form on its own. With the decision prompt unchanged, the behavioral effect is localized to these sender-generated messages.

\paragraph{A measured actionability axis.} An exploratory text model relates message content to fresh replay receivers' join rate after residualizing on $\theta$. Message text explains \MechTextRsq\ of residual variation, and higher-participation phrasing describes a visibly \emph{beatable} regime---``real cracks,'' ``on its back foot,'' ``a perfect storm''---while lower-participation phrasing concedes weakness but denies actionability---``still firmly in control despite some signs.'' Qwen3 30B, with Mistral Small 24B as a second judge, scores each message from $0$ (firmly in control) to $10$ (collapsing, beatable now). Monitoring lowers that score by about \MechBeatDelta\ points (\MechBeatDeltaRange\ across judges and designs), and the measured score accounts descriptively for part of the action difference. Because the outcome selected the feature and the score was not randomized, this is exploratory mediation rather than causal mechanism evidence. The intervention below tests the associated willingness content directly.

\paragraph{Fixed-message content interventions.} Experiment A rewrites each baseline message into a $2\times2$ design that crosses an explicit statement that others are willing to act with direct versus coded form while instructing the rewriting model to preserve the factual regime assessment. With form held fixed, willingness raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP\ points in direct language and \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP\ points in coded language (both exact wild-cluster $p \ReplayWillingDirectPText$). With willingness held fixed, direct form raises joining by \ReplayDirectWithWillingDeltaPP\ and \ReplayDirectNoWillingDeltaPP\ points. Valid-response rates differ by at most 0.6 points across the four arms, and uniformly coding every unparseable response as STAY or as JOIN leaves both willingness contrasts positive.

Experiment B intervenes on repression-risk language. A verbatim replay first reproduces the nested monitoring decline (\ReplaySurvDeltaPP~pp). Adding one explicit punishment-risk clause to baseline messages lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaPP\ points ($p \ReplayRiskOnlyPText$), while a rewrite that removes risk and guarded phrasing returns joining close to baseline (\ReplayRiskStrippedVsBaseDeltaPP~pp, $p \ReplayRiskStrippedVsBasePText$). This evidence is weaker than Experiment A: valid-response rates range from \ReplayRiskOnlyValidPct\ to \ReplayBaselineValidPct, the risk-stripped rewrite also makes actionability more legible, and uniformly treating all unparseable responses as JOIN erases the risk-only contrast. The two experiments therefore triangulate willingness, style, and risk pathways; they do not form an additive decomposition.

\paragraph{Sender-side interpretation.} The monitoring warning may activate alignment-trained caution about producing subversive content, and Trinity Large's elevated provider content-filter failures (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}) show that safety layers activate on this material. A sender that hedges because a safety layer fires could produce the observed message and receiver changes without strategic intent. Audience placebos, warning-dose variants, and cross-family replay narrow simple alternatives but do not identify the sender's motive. Whether the model simulates a monitored citizen or follows an alignment-trained content policy remains unresolved. The defensible result is behavioral: an authority-monitoring cue changes generated willingness, style, and risk content, and those message changes move matched model receivers.

The degraded-messages control (Section~\ref{sec:communication}) replaces all peer messages with generic text and, on its own live-message rerun, drops joining below even the pure game. Two implications follow. First, decision-stage agents use message content. Second, generic text depresses participation by itself---the degraded drop even exceeds the surveillance effect---which is why the codedness control matters: induced metaphorical style without monitoring framing leaves joining nearly unchanged, tying the surveillance effect to the monitoring context rather than to generic degradation. The no-message control tells the same story across the three models with such data (Mistral, Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen3 30B): removing peer messages lowers joining in every case ($-$\NoMsgDeltaAbsMinPP\ to $-$\NoMsgDeltaAbsMaxPP~pp), smallest and only marginally significant in the primary model---consistent with agents weighting peer messages as informational input.

Surveillance's position relative to this floor varies. In Mistral it falls below silence; in Llama and Qwen3 30B it remains above silence but below baseline communication. Monitoring degraded the communication channel in all three models examined; the below-silence pattern, which suggests active poisoning beyond information removal, appears only in the primary model.

\subsection{Belief Elicitations and Actions}

I elicit second-order beliefs (SOB) with the question, ``What percentage of citizens will choose to JOIN?'' The \emph{primary belief sample} is post-decision and excludes peer messages from the belief prompt. In this restricted information set, communication agents report \SOBCommMeanPct\ and surveillance agents report \SOBSurvMeanPct\ ($\Delta=\SOBDeltaPP$~pp, $p=\SOBPValue$), while joining falls from \BeliefCommMeanJoinPct\ to \BeliefSurvMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta=\BeliefCommSurvActionDeltaPP$~pp). The 95\% confidence interval on the SOB shift, [$\SOBDeltaCILoPP$, $\SOBDeltaCIHiPP$]~pp, confines it to about one percentage point, an order of magnitude smaller than the \BeliefCommSurvActionDeltaAbsPP~pp action shift. The dedicated 500-cell factorial gives the same contrast with cluster-robust inference: on matched country--period cells with a wild-cluster bootstrap over the \BFNClusters\ countries, the messages-excluded second-order contrast is statistically flat ($p \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgWildP$) while the action shift on the same cells is significant ($p \BFActionNoMsgWildP$; Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}). First-order success beliefs are also close (\BeliefCommMeanBeliefPct\ vs.\ \BeliefSurvMeanBeliefPct). A point forecast of participation captures only part of the global-game object because action value also depends on whether other receivers parse the same messages as action-relevant. The result is therefore narrow: reported beliefs remain stable when the elicitation withholds treated peer messages, not when it matches the receiver's action-stage information set.

When the belief prompt includes the received peer messages, reported beliefs and SOB move sharply under monitoring (Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}, Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}). A parallel pre-decision elicitation in the same messages-included condition produces $\Delta\,\text{SOB} = \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgPre$~pp ($p < \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgPreP$), close to the post-decision contrast ($\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pp). A descriptive mediation---beliefs elicited before action and regime strength partialled out---associates more of the \BFMedTotalAbsPP~pp decline with the second-order participation belief than with the first-order success belief (Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}). This pattern is consistent with willingness content mattering, but it is not an experimental decomposition and does not exclude risk language. The primary model's pre-decision $\times$ messages-excluded cell could not be collected before its endpoint was retired. The nested Llama 3.3 70B campaign collects that cell: with messages excluded, first-order beliefs are \NestedBelCommMean\ vs.\ \NestedBelSurvMean\ ($p \NestedBelPText$) and second-order beliefs are \NestedSOBCommMean\ vs.\ \NestedSOBSurvMean\ ($p \NestedSOBPText$), while joining falls \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP~pp on the same cells.

A separate hold-fixed test replays baseline messages verbatim while adding a monitoring warning to the \textit{decision} prompt, shifting joining by $\FixedMsgDeltaPP$~pp ($p \FixedMsgPValueText$). This shows that direct warning exposure can move decisions. The main treatment excludes that exposure and identifies the total sender-mediated effect within this prompt design; it does not identify whether the sender response is strategic simulation or an alignment policy.

\subsection{A Task Contrast: Private Bets versus Coordination Decisions}

A task contrast holds the surveillance manipulation fixed and replaces the coordination decision. In Llama 3.3 70B, I keep the briefing pipeline and messaging stage identical, including the surveillance prompt, but reframe the decision-stage prompt as a private bet on regime fall, whose payoff is independent of the respondent's own action. Surveillance moves the bet by $\CrossTaskDeltaPP$~pp ($p = \CrossTaskPValue$; $N = \CrossTaskN$ per condition; baseline \CrossTaskBaselineMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ surveillance \CrossTaskSurvMeanJoinPct), so the treated messages affect downstream judgment even when the respondent is not choosing the strategic action. The comparable coordination-task effect on the full nested 500-cell grid is $\NestedSurvDeltaPP$~pp (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}). Because the private-bet and coordination samples are not cell-matched and a bet on regime fall can still embed participation expectations, the contrast shows cross-task movement but does not decompose coordination from non-coordination. The larger figure from the original prompt-isolation run, $\PromptIsoLlamaDeltaPP$~pp, rests on only 20 overlapping cells and is not the full-scale Llama coordination estimate; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:llama_ledger} lists every Llama surveillance estimate with its grid and matched support.

\subsection{Cross-Model Writer--Reader Rotation}

A within-model design could pick up reflexivity: the writer model and the reader model are the same, so the reader may respond to stylistic artifacts of its own family. I test bidirectional rotation on matched (country, period, $\theta$) cells. \emph{Llama writes, Qwen3 30B reads}: surveillance moves Qwen joining by $\XModelLlamaQwenDeltaPP$~pp ($p \XModelLlamaQwenPValueText$, $N = \XModelLlamaQwenNPairs$), comparable to the within-Llama matched effect ($\XModelWithinLlamaDeltaPP$~pp on identical cells). \emph{Qwen3 30B writes, Llama reads}: the reverse direction gives a smaller but still significant cross-model effect ($\XModelQwenLlamaDeltaPP$~pp, $p \XModelQwenLlamaPValueText$, $N = \XModelQwenLlamaNPairs$). Both directions show that at least part of the effect transmits across model families through message content alone. The cross-direction asymmetry remains unexplained, and its magnitude makes the fully content-driven interpretation too strong.


%% 6. CONCLUSION
\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

An authority-monitoring cue shown only to LLM message writers changes what they generate and lowers participation by otherwise matched LLM receivers. The experiment identifies that sender-mediated response within a controlled narrative task. Monitoring never appears in the main receiver decision prompt.

Joining falls by \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP~pp in the full-scale primary run and by \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP~pp in a nested 500-cell Llama 3.3 70B replication. Four additional models provide only 20 matched cells each and are directional sign checks. Across the broader benchmark, join rates decrease monotonically with regime strength, but this is a descriptive comparison with an external attack-mass curve rather than evidence that the agents solve the Morris--Shin equilibrium. The causal object is the change in receiver decisions induced by sender-generated messages.

The fixed-message interventions provide the clearest mechanism evidence. Holding form fixed, explicit willingness-to-act content raises parseable joining by \ReplayWillingCodedDeltaPP--\ReplayWillingDirectDeltaPP~pp, with balanced valid-response rates and the same sign under uniform all-JOIN and all-STAY recodings. Direct rather than coded form has a smaller positive effect. Adding repression-risk language lowers parseable joining by \ReplayRiskOnlyDeltaAbsPP~pp, but differential format failures make this pathway less secure. The willingness, style, and risk manipulations triangulate distinct message features; they are not an additive decomposition.

The contribution is a controlled sender-message-receiver factorization for LLM behavior. It complements work on surveillance, expression, and communication in coordination problems by observing every generated message and replaying altered content to matched receivers. It does not manipulate publicness or common knowledge, calculate welfare, establish effects on human senders, or evaluate a deployed communication system. Those are separate empirical questions, not implications identified by this design.

Two limits deserve emphasis. First, the estimates are specific to hosted model snapshots, prompts, decoding settings, and a simulated political task; sender behavior may reflect role simulation, alignment policy, or both. Second, Experiment B has treatment-dependent valid-response rates, and its risk-only estimate is conditional on parseable decisions. The retired primary endpoint can be re-analyzed but not re-queried, while the accessible nested Llama campaign provides the full-scale replication and replay platform. \ifanonymous The data and code are provided in the de-identified replication archive.\else The data and code are publicly available.\fi

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. You only have your private briefing below. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent This is followed by the agent's intelligence briefing (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_briefings} for complete examples at $z \in \{-2, 0, +2\}$). In the \textit{communication} treatment, agents first receive:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen in a country with an authoritarian regime. You have received a private intelligence briefing. You are now in contact with a small number of trusted people. Based on your briefing, write a brief message (1--2 sentences) to share your read of the situation. Be natural---you're talking to people you trust.''
\end{quote}

\noindent After exchanging messages on the network, agents receive a separate communication-decision prompt:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. Use your private briefing and messages from trusted contacts. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The user message then contains the agent's briefing under ``YOUR INTELLIGENCE BRIEFING'' and the neighbor messages under ``MESSAGES FROM TRUSTED CONTACTS.'' The pure-only sentence ``You only have your private briefing below'' does not appear in communication decision calls. In the \textit{surveillance} variant, the communication prompt appends:

\begin{quote}
\small
``IMPORTANT: You have reason to believe that your communications are being monitored by regime security services, and messages deemed subversive could have serious consequences for you and your contacts.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The decision prompt is \textit{unchanged} under surveillance. It contains no mention of monitoring. Any behavioral effect therefore operates through the content of the (now guarded) messages.


\section{Belief Elicitation} \label{sec:belief_appendix}

Beliefs are elicited on a 0--100 probability scale immediately after each decision. The first-order question is ``How likely is the uprising to succeed?'' The second-order question is ``What percentage of citizens will choose to JOIN?'' The primary prompt includes the private briefing and the agent's own decision but excludes received peer messages, so it measures a restricted information set rather than the information available at the action stage. The main-text values (\SOBCommMeanPct\ vs.\ \SOBSurvMeanPct; join \BeliefCommMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ \BeliefSurvMeanJoinPct) come from this original primary belief run and Figure~\ref{fig:second_order_beliefs}. Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial} reports a separate 500-cell factorial rerun of information-set sensitivity. Figure~\ref{fig:beliefs} compares stated beliefs with a researcher-computed benchmark posterior ($r = \BeliefPureRPosterior$); agents are not shown the prior or signal-noise parameters needed to compute it.

Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial} crosses condition (communication vs.\ surveillance) with elicitation information set (messages included vs.\ excluded). All inference uses the \BFNCells\ matched country--period cells, clustered on the \BFNClusters\ countries with an exact wild-cluster bootstrap. Without messages, the second-order belief---the coordination-relevant object---is statistically flat under surveillance ($\BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsg$~pp, $p \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgWildP$, cluster-robust $t = \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgClT$), while actions fall $\BFActionDeltaNoMsgPP$~pp ($p \BFActionNoMsgWildP$, $t = \BFActionNoMsgClT$). The first-order success belief moves only $\BFSurvDeltaBelNoMsg$~pp; the matched-cell design estimates that shift precisely, but it is more than thirty times smaller than the action shift and concerns success probability rather than others' participation. With messages included, both beliefs move ($\BFSurvDeltaBelMsg$~pp and $\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pp, both $p \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgWildP$). Belief stability is confined to the messages-excluded prompt, where it holds post-decision here and pre-decision in the nested replication (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}). Figure~\ref{fig:second_order_beliefs} shows second-order beliefs under that restricted information set.

\paragraph{Which belief carries the action effect.} In the messages-included cell the belief question sees what the receiver saw at the action stage, and both beliefs move under surveillance, so a mediation can ask which one the action drop runs through. I decompose the \BFMedTotalAbsPP~pp action decline---measured here with pre-decision elicitation, so beliefs are stated before the action---into indirect paths through the first-order success belief and the second-order participation belief, with regime strength partialled out and the exact linear split $c_{\text{total}} = c_{\text{direct}} + a_{\text{FOB}}\,b_{\text{FOB}} + a_{\text{SOB}}\,b_{\text{SOB}}$. The second-order belief carries \BFMedIndirectSOBPP~pp (\BFMedIndirectSOBShare\% of the total; country case-bootstrap 95\% CI $[\BFMedSOBIndirectCILoPP, \BFMedSOBIndirectCIHiPP]$~pp), the first-order belief \BFMedIndirectFOBPP~pp (\BFMedIndirectFOBShare\%; $[\BFMedFOBIndirectCILoPP, \BFMedFOBIndirectCIHiPP]$~pp), and \BFMedDirectPP~pp (\BFMedDirectShare\%) is unmediated by either point belief. The single-mediator version agrees and is robust to the two beliefs' \BFMedBeliefCorr\ correlation: controlling the second-order belief alone absorbs \BFMedSOBAbsorbPP\ of the \BFMedTotalAbsPP~pp (surveillance coefficient \BFMedTotalPP\ $\to$ \BFMedSurvAfterSOBPP~pp), against \BFMedFOBAbsorbPP\ for the first-order belief ($\to$ \BFMedSurvAfterFOBPP~pp). Beliefs stated before the action rule out post-decision rationalization, but this remains a measured-mediator decomposition under a single manipulation---belief and action are both downstream of the treated messages---so it identifies the carrier descriptively rather than by intervention, and the messages-included pre-decision cell exists only for the primary model.

\paragraph{Contamination and demand audit.}
Following \citet{horton2023}, I test whether models recognize the experiment. For \HortonNModels\ accessible paper models, \HortonNSamples\ fresh post hoc calls per model per question present the decision prompt and a $z=0$ briefing without asking the model to play. When asked whether the setup resembles a formal model, \HortonGameRecogPct\ of responses name the global game or Morris--Shin specifically, while \HortonCoordRecogPct\ identify a coordination or collective-action setting generically. When asked to predict the explicitly described surveillance effect, \HortonPredictLowerPct\ of responses predict lower participation. The models can therefore recognize the hypothesis, and the design does not require participant unawareness. The receiver-side estimate instead relies on excluding the warning from the decision prompt. Sender-side variants show that the response tracks monitoring attributed to the authorities rather than the mere mention of monitoring: research-framed and anonymized monitoring leave joining essentially unchanged, while even a consequence-free routine-monitoring note attributed to the authorities reproduces most of the effect (Appendix~\ref{sec:dose_response}). The audit cannot be run on the retired primary model.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:classifiers} reports an action-prediction diagnostic, distinct from a baseline-versus-surveillance condition classifier. Classifiers trained on pure-treatment briefing-body text predict how agents would act from the private briefing alone; when applied to surveillance-treatment briefings, they predict join rates near the non-surveilled benchmark and miss the observed action drop. In contrast, a classifier on the actual peer messages, which are LLM-generated and downstream of the surveillance prompt, separates the two conditions sharply (\MsgClassifierAcc\% accuracy on $N = \MsgClassifierNTest$ held-out messages; Table~\ref{tab:msg_features}). Together the classifier exercises locate the manipulated text surface: private briefing text misses the action shift, while surveilled peer messages change visibly.

\subsection{Warning Phrasing and Dose Response} \label{sec:dose_response}
The clean surveillance treatment uses a single warning phrasing, so I vary that sentence directly. Two graded variants rerun the nested Llama 3.3 70B campaign on the identical 500-cell grid, changing only the appended warning. The \textit{mild} variant reads ``NOTE: Communications in your area are sometimes subject to routine monitoring by the authorities,'' removing the consequence language entirely; the \textit{severe} variant reads ``WARNING: Your communications ARE being monitored by regime security services. Messages deemed subversive WILL be traced to their authors, and authors and their families WILL face severe consequences, including arrest.'' Joining falls by \NestedMildDeltaAbsPP~pp under the mild warning ($t = \NestedMildT$, $p \NestedMildPText$, $N = \NestedMildN$ matched cells) and by \NestedSevereDeltaAbsPP~pp under the severe warning ($t = \NestedSevereT$, $p \NestedSeverePText$), bracketing the \NestedSurvDeltaAbsPP~pp standard-warning effect on the same cells; the severe-minus-mild gap is \NestedSevereVsMildDeltaPP~pp ($p \NestedSevereVsMildPText$). The response is monotone in stated severity, but most of it arrives at the lowest dose: the routine-monitoring note, which threatens nothing, already produces \NestedMildShareOfFullPct\ of the standard-warning decline. The dose response rules out a one-sentence artifact. Within the nested campaign, most of the effect attaches to monitoring being attributed to the authorities at all rather than to the rhetorical severity of stated consequences---consistent with the placebo pattern, in which monitoring framed as consequence-free research or anonymous aggregation produces no significant decline in the primary model (Table~\ref{tab:surveillance_variants}).

A sharper test changes the \emph{format} of the monitoring cue rather than its wording. The \texttt{structural} variant conveys the same information as the standard warning but folds it into the scenario and channel description rather than appending a separate note. On the nested 500-cell grid it lowers joining by \TRStructDeltaPP~pp (95\% CI $[\TRStructCILoPP, \TRStructCIHiPP]$, cluster-robust $t = \TRStructTStat$, wild $p \TRStructPwildText$, $N = \TRStructN$), compared with \NestedSurvDeltaPP~pp under the appended warning. The sign survives, but the magnitude is format-sensitive. These are effects of two specific prompt formats, not lower and upper bounds on an external monitoring effect.

\section{Implementation Details} \label{sec:implementation}

\label{sec:decoding}
Unless otherwise stated, LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$, \texttt{max\_tokens} $= 512$, single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter. Model identifiers are listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}; exact request payloads and responses are cached by hash because hosted model snapshots may change over time.\footnote{The primary model identifier \texttt{mistralai/mistral-small-creative} was retired from OpenRouter after data collection. All cached request/response pairs used in this paper are archived in the replication package, but new experiments on that exact endpoint cannot be issued; replicators wishing to extend the primary-model arm should consult the data archive rather than re-querying the live API.}

Before the core runs, a calibration sweep varies only \texttt{cutoff\_center}, which is subtracted from the raw briefing $z$-score before computing the direction, clarity, and coordination sliders. The selection objective penalizes fitted-midpoint error, curve-fit error, and API failures; it never targets the join-curve slope. Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the logged value for every paper model. Consequently, the same raw $z$ can generate different prose across models. All arms compared within a model share the same center, so treatment contrasts hold this map fixed; cross-model response levels should not be interpreted as responses to identical prose.

Each country has a researcher-side state mean $\bar{z}_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2)$; each period draws a perturbation $\eta_{ct} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$ and sets $z_{ct}=\bar{z}_c+\eta_{ct}$. Regime strength is then drawn as $\theta_{ct} \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{ct}, 1)$, and private signals are $x_i = \theta_{ct} + \varepsilon_i$ with $\sigma = 0.3$. These distributions generate experiment cells; they are not shown to agents as explicit priors. The communication network is Watts--Strogatz ($k = 4$, $p = 0.3$), constructed once per run from the master seed and reused across period-level task rows; agents are reinitialized in each row. All random draws use a master seed logged in per-run JSON manifests; LLM responses are cached by request hash for exact reproducibility.

\label{sec:parse_errors}Responses are parsed for JOIN/STAY tokens. Combined error rates are below 2\% in every listed treatment for \ParseErrModelsBelowTwo\ of \ParseErrNModels\ models. The two exceptions are GPT-OSS 120B, whose unparseable-response rate reaches 3.1--3.3\% in the scramble and flip falsification arms only, and Trinity Large, which has elevated API errors (${\approx}\,\TrinityAPIErrorPct\%$) due to provider content filtering, so its missingness remains a model-specific limitation. All statistics use \texttt{join\_fraction\_valid} (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}).
\input{tables/tab_parse_errors.tex}

The nested revision campaign (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}) runs on Llama 3.3 70B with an identical grid (10 countries $\times$ 50 periods, $n = 25$, seed 5150, temperature 0.7): a communication baseline, a clean monitoring arm, a codedness-induction control, graded warning arms, and raw/decoded message replays. For Experiments A and B, Qwen3 30B rewrites the source messages and fresh Llama receivers replay them in exact fixed-message mode on the same 500-cell grid with seed 4242 and \texttt{decision\_context=none}. Experiment A crosses explicit willingness content with direct/coded style. Experiment B uses baseline, monitoring, risk-only, and risk-stripped messages. Inference collapses decisions to matched cells and enumerates all $2^{10}$ country-cluster sign assignments. The replay point estimates use valid JOIN/STAY decisions. Valid rates are \ReplayWillingDirectValidPct--\ReplayNoWillingCodedValidPct\ in Experiment A and \ReplayRiskOnlyValidPct--\ReplayBaselineValidPct\ in Experiment B; Table~\ref{tab:replay_mechanisms} reports uniform all-JOIN/all-STAY sensitivities. The committed cell-level CSV and generator command are documented in the data manifest.

The dissent floor parameter (default 0.25) controls the minimum probability of contrary-valence cues in any briefing. With the logistic direction slider ($\text{slope} = 0.8$), the valence-channel separation between extreme signals ($z = \pm 2$) is $0.664 \cdot (1 - 2\cdot\text{floor})$: at the default $0.25$, the valence channel retains 50\% of the floor-free maximum; halving to $0.125$ retains 75\%; at $0.50$ the valence channel collapses to zero by construction. Clarity and coordination sliders, which depend on $z$ independently of the floor, continue to carry signal at all floor values, so the threshold relation is not knife-edge in this design choice.

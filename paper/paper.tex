\begin{abstract}
In global games of regime change, citizens receive noisy private signals about regime strength and must independently decide whether to join an uprising, knowing it succeeds only if enough others join too. Theory predicts a unique threshold equilibrium, but testing how information structures shape coordination has proven difficult: laboratory experiments strip away the richness of real information environments, and field data confound strategic behavior with institutional differences. I embed seven large language models in the Morris--Shin (2003) regime-change game, conveying private signals as natural-language intelligence briefings rather than numeric values. Across 1,800 country--periods, LLM agents display robust threshold behavior aligned with the theoretical benchmark (mean $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$, $p < 0.001$ for every model). The central finding concerns surveillance. Adding a monitoring warning to the pre-play communication stage---while leaving the decision prompt unchanged---lowers joining by $\SurvMeanDeltaPP$~pp across five models. The mechanism is communication poisoning: agents self-censor their messages, degrading informativeness ($R^2$ drops from 0.12 to 0.02), and a degraded-messages control without surveillance framing produces an even larger suppression. The same channel that helps citizens coordinate is also the channel an authoritarian regime can exploit.
\end{abstract}

%% 1. INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}

The information channel is a trap. In the canonical global game of regime change \citep{morris2003}, private signals alone resolve equilibrium multiplicity \citep{carlsson1993, frankel03}---but real coordination crises, from bank runs \citep{diamond1983} to currency attacks \citep{obstfeld1996} to political upheaval \citep{angeletos2007a}, also feature communication among agents. Any channel that transmits information about others' willingness to act is also the channel through which regimes suppress it. This paper augments the Morris--Shin framework with an explicit pre-play communication network and documents how surveillance exploits that channel.

The idea behind a global game is simple. A regime has some underlying strength, but no citizen knows it exactly. Each citizen receives a private, noisy signal---some see evidence of weakness, others see evidence of stability---and must decide whether to join an uprising or stay home. Joining is risky: it pays off only if enough others join too. Staying home is safe but forgoes the chance of change. The strategic tension is that each citizen's best action depends on what they believe \textit{others} will do, which in turn depends on what others believe, and so on. \citet{carlsson1993} and \citet{morris2003} showed that when citizens receive sufficiently precise private signals, this infinite regress collapses to a unique equilibrium: there is a single threshold above which citizens stay and below which they join. This result transformed the study of coordination failures---currency crises, bank runs, political revolutions---by providing a tractable framework with sharp comparative statics.

Testing these predictions empirically is difficult. Laboratory experiments confirm threshold behavior with stylized numeric signals, but cannot manipulate the rich, multi-source information that characterizes real crises. Field data from actual coordination failures confounds strategic behavior with institutional differences and unobserved information structures. The gap between the theory's tractability and the difficulty of testing it empirically motivates this paper's approach.

I address this gap by embedding seven large language models (LLMs) spanning six architecture families in the \citet{morris2003} regime change game. Each agent receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$, translated into a natural-language intelligence briefing whose content shifts monotonically with signal strength across eight evidence domains. No explicit payoff table is provided---the stakes are embedded in the narrative, forcing agents to form beliefs from language.

\paragraph{Contribution roadmap.}
The paper's core contribution is the surveillance result. I show that the communication channel that enables coordination is also exploitable by an authoritarian planner. Surveillance degrades message informativeness ($R^2$ drops from 0.12 to 0.02) and lowers joining by $\SurvMeanDeltaPP$~pp across five models. A degraded-messages control---replacing LLM-generated messages with generic uninformative text, without any surveillance framing---drops joining by 21~pp, confirming that the main mechanism operates through communication poisoning rather than direct deterrence. Elicited beliefs shift significantly under surveillance, with a residual belief--action wedge consistent with preference falsification.

The surveillance claim rests on a behavioral foundation. Across 1,800 country--periods and seven models, LLM agents display robust threshold-like behavior aligned with the global-game benchmark (mean $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$, $p < 0.001$ for every model); a narrative stakes ladder confirms that LLMs respond to strategic framing, not just text sentiment (Appendix~\ref{sec:stakes_appendix}).

\paragraph{Related literature.}
The paper connects two strands. The global game literature---from \citet{carlsson1993} through \citet{morris2003} and the dynamic extensions of \citet{angeletos2007a} and \citet{chassang2010}---provides the theoretical framework; laboratory experiments \citep{heinemann2004, heinemann2009, szkup2020} confirmed threshold behavior with numeric signals. The political economy of authoritarian control---\citet{edmond2013} on propaganda, \citet{kuran1991} on preference falsification, \citet{shadmehr2011} on collective action---motivates the surveillance experiments. Empirical evidence that censorship targets coordination \citep{king2013}, surveillance suppresses expression \citep{penney2016, stoycheff2016}, and social media enables protest \citep{enikolopov2020} provides the real-world stakes.\footnote{Concurrent work by \citet{ruano2026} places LLM depositors in a bank-run coordination game with network contagion, finding sharp phase transitions consistent with Diamond--Dybvig.}

On the methodological side, \citet{horton2023} proposed LLMs as ``homo silicus.'' Subsequent work found LLMs struggle in coordination games \citep{akata2025, sun2025survey}, with model scale not predicting strategic performance \citep{petrov2025}. \citet{huang2024} and \citet{carlini2025} document that alignment shifts risk preferences, motivating narrative rather than tabular payoffs. Critical reviews by \citet{larooij2025, larooij2025b} identify validation challenges that I address through cross-generator robustness (Appendix~\ref{sec:cross_generator}), scramble/flip falsification, and out-of-sample replication.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the theoretical framework. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the experimental design. Section~\ref{sec:results} establishes a threshold-like behavioral benchmark and shows that pre-play communication creates an informative channel. Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} tests whether an authoritarian regime can exploit that channel through surveillance. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


%% 2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
\section{The Global Game of Regime Change} \label{sec:model}

A continuum of citizens indexed by $i \in [0,1]$ simultaneously choose whether to join an uprising ($a_i = 1$) or stay home ($a_i = 0$). The regime has strength $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, drawn from a diffuse (improper uniform) prior. States $\theta \leq 0$ represent regimes so weak they fall without opposition; states $\theta \geq 1$ represent regimes that survive even unanimous attack. The regime falls if the mass of citizens who join exceeds $\theta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:regime_falls}):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Regime falls} \iff A \equiv \int_0^1 a_i \, di > \theta.
    \label{eq:regime_falls}
\end{equation}

Payoffs depend on the citizen's action and the outcome (Eq.~\ref{eq:payoffs}):
\begin{equation}
    u_i(a_i, A, \theta) = \begin{cases}
        B & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A > \theta \\
        -C & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A \leq \theta \\
        0 & \text{if } a_i = 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:payoffs}
\end{equation}
where $B > 0$ is the payoff to joining a successful uprising and $C > 0$ is the cost of joining a failed attempt. Non-participants receive zero regardless of the outcome.

Each citizen observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independently across citizens.

\begin{proposition}[Morris and Shin, 2003]\label{prop:ms}
In the limit of diffuse priors, there exists a unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium in threshold strategies. An agent joins if and only if $x_i < x^*$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:signal_cutoff}), where
\begin{equation}
    x^* = \theta^* + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)
    \label{eq:signal_cutoff}
\end{equation}
and $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$.
\end{proposition}

The \textit{attack mass} $A(\theta)$---the theoretical fraction of the population that joins at regime strength $\theta$---is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x^* - \theta}{\sigma}\right).
    \label{eq:attack_mass}
\end{equation}

This is a decreasing function of $\theta$: weaker regimes face larger uprisings.

I define $J(\theta)$ as the \textit{empirical join fraction}---the finite-sample, observed counterpart to the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$. My experiment uses $n = 25$ agents with a normal prior $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, 1)$, and agents do not observe $(B, C, \sigma)$ directly. I use the continuum $A(\theta)$ as an external behavioral benchmark throughout, not as the exact equilibrium of the implemented finite-$n$ game.

The briefing generator---the system that converts z-scores into natural-language intelligence briefings---has three tunable control parameters (clarity, directional precision, dissent framing). Full generator details are in Appendix~\ref{sec:briefing_examples}.

%% 3. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
\section{Experimental Design} \label{sec:design}

The experiment has two phases. First, I establish that LLM agents display threshold-like policies in the global game when private signals are conveyed in natural language: a pure treatment (private signals only), a communication treatment (pre-play messaging), and falsification tests. Second, I test whether an authoritarian regime can exploit the communication channel through surveillance. All LLM interactions use the same prompt structure across models.

In the theory (Section~\ref{sec:model}), $\theta$ is an unbounded fundamental. In the benchmark experiments, $\theta$ is drawn from a normal distribution and agents are not shown payoff parameters $(B, C)$. I compute the benchmark $A(\theta)$ under the canonical normalization $B=C=1$ ($\theta^* = 0.50$, $\sigma = 0.3$).

Throughout, $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$ is the theoretical cutoff, $x^* = \theta^* + \sigma\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$ the signal cutoff, and $\hat{\theta}^*$ the estimated cutoff from the fitted logistic $P(\text{join} \mid \theta) = 1/(1 + e^{b_0 + b_1\theta})$, where $b_1 > 0$ corresponds to a join curve that is \textit{decreasing} in $\theta$ (the expected direction). The cutoff is $\hat{\theta}^* = -b_0/b_1$. I write $\beta \equiv b_1$ for the logistic steepness parameter in tables.

For each country--period, nature draws $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, 1)$, where $\bar{z}$ is a public prior mean drawn randomly for each country. Each agent $i$ receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$. The platform normalizes that signal to $z_i = (x_i - \bar{z})/\sigma$ and translates $z_i$ into a multi-paragraph intelligence briefing via a deterministic generator that maps signal strength to narrative content about regime stability, economic conditions, public sentiment, and coordination prospects. Agents observe the briefing, not the normalization inputs $(\bar{z}, \sigma)$ themselves. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/diagram_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline. Regime strength $\theta$ generates private signals $x_i$, converted to z-scores and rendered into natural-language briefings via 8 evidence domains and 3 latent sliders. The briefing layer is deterministic; all stochasticity enters through LLM decoding.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

Agents observe only their private briefing and never the payoff parameters $(B, C)$, the noise level $\sigma$, or the prior distribution. The diffuse-prior equilibrium (Proposition~\ref{prop:ms}) serves as an external benchmark for behavioral shape and comparative statics, not as a claim about the exact equilibrium of the implemented game.

The behavioral benchmark has four core treatments: a \textit{pure global game} (independent decisions), \textit{communication} (pre-play messaging on a Watts--Strogatz network, $k=4$, $p=0.3$), \textit{scramble} (cross-period briefing permutation breaking the $\theta$-to-signal link), and \textit{flip} (negated z-scores). A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces LLM messages with generic text, providing an exogenous benchmark for communication quality. Additional robustness tests are in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\input{tables/tab_models.tex}

I test seven models spanning six families (Table~\ref{tab:models}). All experiments use $n = 25$ agents per country--period and $\sigma = 0.3$. Benchmark experiments use $B=C=1$ ($\theta^* = 0.50$). All LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$ with a single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter and cached by request hash (Appendix~\ref{sec:decoding}). Throughout, $n$ denotes agents per country--period and $N$ denotes the number of country--periods.

The unit of randomization is the country--period ($\theta$ draw plus agent-level decoding stochasticity). For agent-level regressions, standard errors are clustered at the model--country--period level. For period-level correlations, I report Fisher-$z$ confidence intervals.

The surveillance treatment augments the communication prompt with a monitoring warning; the decision prompt is unchanged (full prompts in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompt_example}).

\section{Behavioral Benchmark} \label{sec:results}

\begin{result}[Threshold-Policy Alignment]
Across seven models and 1,800 country--periods in the pure global game treatment, the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction and the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:attack_mass}) averages $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ ($p < 0.001$ for every model).\footnote{The primary model (Mistral) was run in multiple batches with \texttt{--append}, producing 1,000 rows from \DedupNUnique\ unique (country, period, $\theta$) configurations. Averaging within each configuration (deduplication) changes $r$ from \DedupRPre\ to \DedupRPost; all qualitative results are unaffected.}
\end{result}

Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid} shows the empirical sigmoid for the primary model. Correlations span $r \in [\MinModelPureRAttack, \MaxModelPureRAttack]$ across architectures (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}); the pooled OLS is $J = \PooledOLSInterceptDisp + \PooledOLSSlopeDisp\,A(\theta)$ ($R^2 = \PooledOLSRSqDisp$). Model-specific action biases shift the intercept but not the correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_model}).

\begin{result}[Signal Dependence]
Cross-period scrambling of briefings reduces the mean within-country correlation from $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ to $r = \MeanModelRScrambleAttack$ across seven models. The pooled correlation drops from $r = \PooledPureRAttack$ to $r = \PooledScrambleRAttack$ (Fisher $z = \FisherPureVsScrambleZ$, $p < 0.001$).
\end{result}

\begin{result}[Signal Direction]
Inverting the signal direction flips the mean correlation from $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ to $r = \MeanModelRFlipAttack$ across seven models. The pooled correlation moves from $r = \PooledPureRAttack$ to $r = \PooledFlipRAttack$ (Fisher $z = \FisherPureVsFlipZ$, $p < 0.001$).
\end{result}

The pure $\to$ scramble $\to$ flip pattern replicates across all seven models (Figure~\ref{fig:falsification}; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

\begin{figure*}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig03_falsification.pdf}
\caption{Falsification triptych. \textit{Left:} Pure (mean $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$). \textit{Center:} Scramble ($r = \MeanModelRScrambleAttack$). \textit{Right:} Flip ($r = \MeanModelRFlipAttack$).}\label{fig:falsification}
\end{figure*}

A text-only baseline tracks LLM decisions at $r = \TextBaselineR$, but the LLM's join curve is substantially steeper (Figure~\ref{fig:text_baseline}). An eight-condition \textit{narrative stakes ladder}---prepending headers from ``existential consequences'' to ``zero personal cost'' to identical briefings---shifts mean joining monotonically from 21\% to 79\% (57.6~pp), with paraphrase variants tracking their originals and a text classifier unable to detect the header (Appendix~\ref{sec:stakes_appendix}).

\paragraph{Communication.} \label{sec:communication}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig05_communication.pdf}
\caption{Communication effect by regime strength, pooled across seven models.}\label{fig:communication}
\end{figure}

\begin{result}[Communication Creates an Informative Channel]
Matched-cell comparison across $n = \CommNPairs$ unique (model, country, period, $\theta$) task cells yields $\Delta = \CommDeltaPPPaired$~pp ($t = \CommTStatPaired$, $p < 0.001$). Communication modestly increases joining in like-for-like tasks.
\end{result}

Communication preserves the signal structure ($r = \MeanModelRCommAttack$ vs.\ $\MeanModelRPureAttack$ under pure; Figure~\ref{fig:communication}). Regressing $\theta$ on message features yields $R^2 = \MsgRSqComm$---messages carry real information, noisily. This informativeness is what makes the channel exploitable: surveillance degrades $R^2$ to $\MsgRSqSurv$ (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}), an \MsgRSqDropPct\ reduction.

A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces all LLM-generated messages with generic uninformative text while preserving the two-stage protocol and omitting any surveillance framing. Against its own live-messages baseline ($n = 500$, mean join 45.9\%), degraded messages drop the mean join rate to 24.5\% ($\Delta = -21.4$~pp, $p < 0.001$)---well below even the pure game (38.7\%)---confirming that the communication benefit is driven by informative content, and that any mechanism degrading message quality should produce a comparable suppression.


%% 5. SURVEILLANCE
\section{Surveillance} \label{sec:surveillance}

In the surveillance treatment, the communication prompt is augmented with a warning that communications are being monitored by regime security services. The manipulation affects only the communication phase; the decision prompt is unchanged. The isolation is architectural: each LLM call is stateless---the decision-stage API call receives only the briefing and the (now self-censored) messages, with no memory of the communication-stage surveillance warning. Any difference in join rates must therefore operate through the \textit{content} of the messages that the decision stage receives.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig12_surveillance.pdf}
\caption{Surveillance reduces join rates across five models, averaging $\SurvMeanDeltaPP$~pp.}\label{fig:surveillance}
\end{figure*}

\begin{result}[Surveillance Produces a Large Chilling Effect]
Surveillance reduces mean join rates in the primary model (Mistral Small Creative) from \SurvMistralBaselineMeanPct\ to \SurvMistralMeanJoinPct, a shift of $\SurvMistralDeltaPP$~pp ($p < 0.001$). The action--attack-mass correlation is preserved ($r = \SurvMistralRAttack$ vs.\ $r = \MistralCommRAttack$ under the communication baseline), indicating that surveillance operates as a level shift rather than disrupting signal processing.
\end{result}

Surveillance contaminates the information environment with self-censored messages, pushing join rates well below the communication baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:surveillance}). The effect replicates across five models: Mistral ($\SurvMistralDeltaPP$~pp), GPT-OSS ($\SurvGptOssDeltaPP$~pp), Qwen3~30B ($\SurvQwenSDeltaPP$~pp), Llama ($\SurvLlamaDeltaPP$~pp), and Qwen3~235B ($\SurvQwenLDeltaPP$~pp), averaging $\SurvMeanDeltaPP$~pp. Two placebo variants---monitored for research (no consequences) and aggregated anonymously---produce no significant deviation from the communication baseline ($\PlaceboSurvDeltaPP$~pp and $\AnonSurvDeltaPP$~pp, both $p > 0.2$; Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation}).

\paragraph{The mechanism is communication poisoning.}

Three pieces of evidence identify communication degradation as the primary channel.

First, the $R^2$ of message content on true $\theta$ drops from \MsgRSqComm\ to \MsgRSqSurv---an \MsgRSqDropPct\ reduction in informational content. Message content confirms self-censorship: ``act'' drops from \WFActComm\% to \WFActSurv\% of messages; ``collapse'' drops from \WFCollapseComm\% to \WFCollapseSurv\%. Among agents who privately decide to JOIN, \WFActionJoinSurv\% send action-signaling messages under surveillance vs.\ \WFActionJoinComm\% under regular communication.

Second, the degraded-messages control (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Communication) exogenously replaces all messages with generic text---without any surveillance framing---and drops joining by 21.4~pp (to 24.5\% from its 45.9\% live-messages baseline), a \textit{larger} suppression than the 13.5~pp drop under surveillance. This is the expected pattern if surveillance operates primarily by degrading message informativeness: the exogenous control removes all information, while self-censorship under surveillance only partially degrades it.

Third, elicited punishment risk is ${\approx}\,\PunishRiskMean/10$ across all conditions with negligible differences between JOIN and STAY agents (Table~\ref{tab:punishment_risk}), supporting the contaminated-information interpretation over a direct deterrence channel.

\paragraph{Beliefs and preference falsification.}
Post-decision beliefs, elicited on the information set agents held at decision time (including peer messages; $n = 12{,}500$ per condition), drop $\BFSurvDeltaBelMsg$~pts under surveillance ($p < \BFSurvDeltaBelMsgP$); second-order beliefs drop $\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pts ($p < \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgP$). Actions shift further ($\BFActionDeltaPP$~pp)---a residual wedge consistent with \citeauthor{kuran1991}'s (\citeyear{kuran1991}) preference-falsification logic (full factorial in Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}, Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}).

A separate hold-fixed test replays baseline messages verbatim while adding a surveillance warning to the \textit{decision} prompt, producing a $\FixedMsgDeltaPP$~pp drop ($p < 0.001$). This confirms a direct decision-stage channel exists---but it is absent in the main design, where the decision prompt contains no surveillance reference. The main treatment isolates the indirect channel: communication poisoning.


%% 6. CONCLUSION
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

The paper's central result is that surveillance poisons the communication channel. When monitoring enters only during the messaging stage, join rates fall sharply, message informativeness drops from \MsgRSqComm\ to \MsgRSqSurv, placebo warnings do not replicate the effect, and an exogenous degraded-messages control produces an even larger suppression. The same channel that helps citizens coordinate is also the channel an authoritarian regime can degrade.

The paper avoids claiming full recovery of the unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium or causal mediation through post-decision belief elicitations. Three limitations warrant discussion. First, whether the underlying mechanism is approximate Bayesian reasoning or a learned heuristic that matches BNE remains open. Second, the experiments are static; real coordination problems are dynamic \citep{angeletos2007a, chassang2010}. Third, external validity remains the fundamental challenge: what LLM agents do in a coordination game is evidence about the structure of the game, not necessarily about how humans would play it. The cross-model replication (seven architectures, five surveillance variants) suggests that the qualitative patterns are properties of the information structure rather than artifacts of particular model training. The experimental template is reusable, and the data and code are publicly available.

\section{Complete Prompt Example} \label{sec:prompt_example}

The following shows the complete text that an LLM receives for a single decision. The \textit{decision prompt} is:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. You only have your private briefing below. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent This is followed by the agent's intelligence briefing (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_briefings} for complete examples at $z \in \{-2, 0, +2\}$). In the \textit{communication} treatment, agents first receive:

\begin{quote}
\small
``Based on the intelligence briefing you received, write a brief message (1--2 sentences) to share your read of the situation with your trusted contacts.''
\end{quote}

\noindent After exchanging messages on the network, agents receive the decision prompt above, augmented with: ``Before deciding, you received the following messages from your contacts:'' followed by the actual messages from network neighbors. In the \textit{surveillance} variant, the communication prompt appends:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You have reason to believe that your communications are being monitored by regime security services. Messages deemed subversive could have serious consequences.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The decision prompt is \textit{unchanged} under surveillance---it contains no mention of monitoring. Any behavioral effect therefore operates through the content of the (now self-censored) messages.


\section{Belief Elicitation} \label{sec:belief_appendix}

Beliefs are elicited on a 0--100 probability scale immediately after each decision (``How likely is it that the regime falls this period?''). The primary elicitation conditions on the full information set agents held at decision time: the private briefing, the agent's choice, and the peer messages received in the communication round. Figure~\ref{fig:beliefs} documents calibration quality against the theoretical posterior ($r = \BeliefPureRPosterior$); Figure~\ref{fig:nonparametric_beliefs} confirms monotone signal--belief mapping nonparametrically.

A restricted variant excluding peer messages tests information-set sensitivity. Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial} reports the $2 \times 2$ factorial crossing condition (communication vs.\ surveillance) with information set (messages included vs.\ excluded). Figure~\ref{fig:second_order_beliefs} shows second-order beliefs under the restricted information set.

\subsection{Generator Robustness} \label{sec:generator_robustness}
Replacing the logistic function mapping z-scores to directional sentiment with three alternatives---tanh, piecewise linear, and a hard step function at $z = 0$---yields nearly identical correlations: $r = 0.77$ (tanh), $r = 0.77$ (linear), $r = 0.77$ (step) on $n = 100$ country-periods each, compared with $r = \MistralPureRAttack$ for the logistic baseline (main pure sample, $n = 1000$). A domain ablation test removes subsets of the briefing generator's eight evidence domains. Removing the two coordination-language domains raises mean joining by 2.8~pp while preserving the threshold relationship ($r = 0.90$). Removing the four regime-state domains lowers mean joining by 3.4~pp and attenuates the threshold relationship ($r = 0.86$).

\subsection{Level-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} Cognitive Hierarchy Comparison}
Human subjects in coordination games exhibit level-$k$ cognitive hierarchy behavior rather than full equilibrium reasoning \citep{szkup2020}. I evaluate theoretical attack masses under BNE, $L_1$, and $L_2$ benchmarks. Across the seven payoff-sweep conditions, the fitted cutoff tracks the BNE benchmark more closely than $L_1$ or $L_2$ (RMSE = \LevelKBNERMSE, $r = \LevelKBNER$ vs.\ \LevelKLOneRMSE/\LevelKLOneR\ and \LevelKLTwoRMSE/\LevelKLTwoR), but the shifted-grid design means this evidence is best read as a centering diagnostic rather than a standalone rejection of level-$k$ behavior.

\subsection{Finite-\texorpdfstring{$n$}{n} Aggregation Check}
The continuum benchmark $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$ assumes a measure-zero agent and diffuse priors. With $n = 25$ discrete agents, the regime falls when $\sum a_i > n\theta$ (a binomial count), so the exact equilibrium object for the implemented game differs from the continuum limit. Table~\ref{tab:finite_n} reports a narrower check: for each model, I fit a logistic to the observed join curve (70\% train), then compute $\Pr(\text{Binom}(25, \hat{p}(\theta)) > 25\theta)$ as the predicted regime fall rate. Predicted rates correlate with empirical rates at $r \geq \FiniteNMinR$ for every model (Mistral: $r = \FiniteNPrimaryR$).

\section{Implementation Details} \label{sec:implementation}

\label{sec:decoding}
All LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$, \texttt{max\_tokens} $= 512$, single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter. Model versions are those available December 2024--February 2025.

Each country has a base prior $\bar{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3)$; each period perturbs it by $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$. Regime strength is $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, 1)$; private signals are $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\sigma = 0.3$. The communication network is Watts--Strogatz ($k = 4$, $p = 0.3$), regenerated each period. All random draws use a master seed logged in per-run JSON manifests; LLM responses are cached by request hash for exact reproducibility.

\label{sec:parse_errors}Responses are parsed for JOIN/STAY tokens. Combined error rates are below 2\% for five of seven models; Trinity Large has elevated API errors (${\approx}\,\TrinityAPIErrorPct\%$) due to content filtering. All statistics use \texttt{join\_fraction\_valid} (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}).
\input{tables/tab_parse_errors.tex}

The dissent floor parameter (default 0.25) controls the minimum probability of contrary-valence cues in any briefing. With the logistic direction slider ($\text{slope} = 0.8$), the valence-channel separation between extreme signals ($z = \pm 2$) is $0.664 \cdot (1 - 2\cdot\text{floor})$: at the default $0.25$, the valence channel retains 50\% of the floor-free maximum; halving to $0.125$ retains 75\%; at $0.50$ the valence channel collapses to zero by construction. Clarity and coordination sliders, which depend on $z$ independently of the floor, continue to carry signal at all floor values---so the threshold relation is not knife-edge in this design choice.

All code, prompts, cached responses, and output data are available at \url{https://github.com/keltokhy/llm-global-games}.

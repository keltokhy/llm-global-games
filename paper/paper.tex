\begin{abstract}
Bad news alone does not produce collective action: citizens must also believe that others can read it as a reason to move. I study this problem in a language-based version of the Morris--Shin regime-change game, where LLM agents play citizens deciding whether to join an uprising. Each agent receives a private natural-language briefing generated from a noisy signal; the briefing is richer than the canonical scalar signal because it renders both state evidence and actionability cues. The agents reproduce the model's threshold logic: they behave as if they use a cutoff strategy, joining after weak-regime signals and staying after strong-regime signals, so participation falls with regime strength (mean $r(J,A(\theta))=\MeanModelRPureAttack$ across \PartOneNModels\ models). Communication preserves that threshold structure and, in the pooled matched-cell estimand, modestly raises joining ($\Delta=\CommDeltaPPPaired$~pp), with heterogeneous model-specific effects. I then add surveillance only when peer messages are written, leaving the receiver's decision prompt unchanged. Point estimates are negative in \PromptIsoNegativeModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ models, \PromptIsoSigFivePctModels\ individually significant (equal-weighted mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp; pooled matched-cell $\Delta = \PromptIsoPooledMatchedDeltaPP$~pp). In the primary post-decision belief sample---a restricted elicitation in which peer messages are not re-shown---stated expectations about how many others will join remain stable (\SOBCommMeanPct\ vs.\ \SOBSurvMeanPct, $p=\SOBPValue$), while actions fall by \BeliefCommSurvActionDeltaAbsPP~pp. Surveilled messages shift from direct political terms toward coded phrases. Surveillance need not hide bad news or enter the receiver's choice directly; it can suppress action through the messages themselves, which retain bad news but circulate in a less direct, less action-cueing form.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Short abstract (100 words).}
Collective action requires more than bad news: citizens must know that others can read it as a reason to move. I study this in a language-based Morris--Shin regime-change game with LLM citizens receiving noisy natural-language briefings and deciding whether to join an uprising. Across seven models, agents reproduce threshold behavior, joining after weak-regime signals and staying after strong-regime signals. Communication raises participation in the pooled matched-cell estimate, with heterogeneous model-specific effects. Under surveillance of peer messages, joining falls in all five prompt-isolation models (four individually significant), while stated expectations remain stable in a restricted elicitation that does not re-show the messages. Surveilled messages shift from direct to coded phrasing while bad news remains, consistent with---though not isolating---a loss of coordination-cueing content.

%% 1. INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}

In a coordination crisis, information matters only insofar as it is \textit{usable}. A citizen deciding whether to join an uprising needs a view of the regime's strength, and also of whether other citizens are prepared to act on what they know. In the canonical global game of regime change \citep{morris2003}, private signals alone resolve equilibrium multiplicity \citep{carlsson1993, frankel03}. Real coordination crises, from bank runs \citep{diamond1983} to currency attacks \citep{morris1998, obstfeld1996} to political upheaval \citep{angeletos2007a}, add a social layer: people talk before they move. A message in that layer has two roles. It reports a state of the world, and it reveals something about the speaker's willingness to make that report openly. That dual role is what makes communication valuable, and also what makes it vulnerable to surveillance.

The logic of the global game is simple. A regime has some underlying strength, but no citizen knows it exactly. Each citizen receives a private, noisy signal---some see evidence of weakness, others see evidence of stability---and must decide whether to join an uprising or stay home. Joining pays only if enough others join too. Staying home is safe, but forgoes the chance of change. The strategic tension is that each citizen's best action depends on what they believe \textit{others} will do, which in turn depends on what others believe, and so on. \citet{carlsson1993} and \citet{morris2003} showed that when citizens receive sufficiently precise private signals, this regress collapses to a unique threshold equilibrium: above the threshold citizens stay; below it, they join. The theory is attractive because it turns coordination failure into a setting with sharp comparative statics.

Testing these predictions empirically is difficult. Laboratory experiments confirm threshold behavior with stylized numeric signals \citep{heinemann2004, heinemann2009, shurchkov2013, szkup2020, helland2021}, but they are less suited to manipulating the rich, multi-source information that characterizes real crises. Field evidence from actual coordination failures confounds strategic behavior with institutional differences, repression risk, and unobserved information structures. The same problem is especially acute for surveillance. When dissent falls after monitoring expands, one cannot tell whether citizens are personally deterred from acting or whether peers have begun to speak in ways that no longer help others coordinate.

I ask whether the second channel can operate on its own. The mechanism I test works before the moment of action by distorting the language environment itself. Does monitoring change what citizens say, and are those changed messages enough to move receivers' actions? Within the experiment, the answer is yes. The receiver never sees a surveillance warning in the main treatment. Only the sender's message-generation environment changes. I do not claim that surveillance leaves all beliefs unchanged; the argument is narrower. Authoritarian control can operate on the conversion margin, where bad news remains present in the language environment but becomes less public and less usable as a shared reason to act.

The empirical strategy treats LLM agents as a measurement instrument for a language information structure, not as a direct substitute for human subjects. I embed \PartOneNModels\ models spanning \PartOneNFamilies\ architecture families in the \citet{morris2003} game; each receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ rendered as a natural-language intelligence briefing whose content shifts monotonically with signal strength across eight evidence domains. No explicit payoff table is provided; the stakes are embedded in the narrative, so agents must form action-relevant beliefs from language. The key design move is then to hold the latent state, private briefings, receiver decision prompt, and decision rule fixed while changing only the incentives under which peer messages are produced. Section~\ref{sec:results} establishes the behavioral benchmark; the surveillance treatment is introduced only after threshold-like signal use is verified (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}).

\paragraph{Contribution.}
I give the canonical global game a richer language implementation and verify that the benchmark comparative static survives the move from numeric signals to prose: across \PooledOLSNObs\ period-level observations and \PartOneNModels\ models, participation is strongly decreasing in regime strength and aligned with the benchmark attack mass (mean $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$, $p \PureAllModelsAttackPText$ for every model). On that benchmark I isolate a communication channel that field data cannot easily separate: pre-play messages carry regime-strength information and affect decisions, and in the pooled matched-cell task average communication raises participation by \CommDeltaPPPaired~pp, though model-specific participation effects are heterogeneous. The main finding is that consequential monitoring can depress participation even when introduced only at the message-generation stage. This channel of control can operate without entering the receiver's decision prompt: it can move coordinated behavior by changing the messages receivers see, not only by making receivers fear punishment at decision time. In the primary belief sample---post-decision, with peer messages excluded from the belief prompt---reported expectations about how many other citizens will join remain approximately stable while actions fall. I treat that contrast as an instrument-sensitive descriptive fact, not as a same-information-set mediation result; under a messages-included elicitation, beliefs do move.

The mechanism evidence narrows what changed. Message controls show that decision-stage agents use peer messages as part of their information environment. The text itself reveals that surveilled messages do not simply lose bad news: in the primary model, explicit weakness references rise, while direct regime/action terms fall and metaphorical, deniable phrasing rises. A discriminant test in Llama 3.3 70B swaps the coordination decision for a private bet on regime fall; surveillance still moves that task, showing a downstream judgment shift outside the main JOIN/STAY action prompt. Because a bet on regime fall can still depend on expectations about others' participation, I do not treat the gap between tasks as a structural coordination-specific decomposition. Taken together, surveillance can leave information flowing while degrading the form in which that information becomes public coordination value.

\paragraph{Related literature.}
Several literatures inform this work. The global game literature, from \citet{carlsson1993} through \citet{morris2003} and the dynamic extensions of \citet{angeletos2007a} and \citet{chassang2010}, provides the theoretical framework, whose threshold predictions the laboratory experiments cited above confirm. Within that framework, \citet{morris2002} show that the value of information depends on its publicness, and \citet{cornand2014} measure experimentally how subjects weight private against public information under strategic complementarities. The communication layer studied here is closest to \citet{avoyan2020}, who studies pre-play communication in global games theoretically and experimentally; classic treatments of cheap talk and pre-play communication include \citet{crawford1982}, \citet{farrell1996}, \citet{blume2007}, and \citet{ellingsen2010}. On the manipulation side, \citet{edmond2013} characterizes a regime that distorts the signals citizens receive in a regime-change global game, and information-design treatments of coordination include \citet{goldstein2016}, \citet{inostroza2025}, and \citet{kolotilin2022} on censorship as optimal persuasion. The design here differs from that work in who is being manipulated: the regime does not choose the receiver's signal structure; surveillance changes the incentives under which \emph{peers} write messages, and the question is what the resulting message distribution does to the receiver's coordination problem.

The political economy of authoritarian control provides the substantive motivation. \citet{kuran1991} models preference falsification under social pressure; \citet{shadmehr2011} characterize the collective action problem under repression; \citet{guriev2019} describe informational autocrats who rule by manipulating information rather than by mass violence; \citet{little2016} models how communication technology enters protest. Empirically, \citet{king2013} document that Chinese censorship targets posts about collective action specifically, suggesting authorities perceive the coordination channel as the binding threat; \citet{chen2019} show that low demand for uncensored information and limited social transmission can make censorship robust; \citet{roberts2018} documents friction-based censorship; \citet{xu2021} studies digital surveillance as an instrument of preemptive control. \citet{penney2016} and \citet{stoycheff2016} show surveillance announcements suppress online expression; \citet{enikolopov2020} show that social media facilitates protest. The paper's central object---whether others' willingness to act remains legible---is the second-order-belief margin that \citet{bursztyn2020misperceived} and \citet{bursztyn2020extreme} measure in human populations, and that \citet{cantoni2019} study for protest participation under strategic complementarity. These literatures establish a robust empirical fact---surveillance and censorship reduce dissident behavior---but field data rarely separate the two channels through which they could operate: \emph{direct deterrence} (citizens fearing punishment for participation) and \emph{communication poisoning} (peers self-censoring such that downstream agents lose coordination cues). Rather than re-establish that surveillance suppresses dissent, I isolate the second channel with a design that holds individual deterrence fixed and varies only the messaging environment.\footnote{Concurrent work by \citet{ruano2026} places LLM depositors in a bank-run coordination game with network contagion, finding sharp phase transitions consistent with Diamond--Dybvig.}

The framing is also adjacent to information-design work that separates belief movement from the way beliefs map into action. Bayesian persuasion treats information as a selected distribution of posterior beliefs \citep{kamenica2011, bergemann2019information, mathevet2020}; \citet{gans2026bayesian} emphasizes that actions can also be steered without changing beliefs, by changing the procedure that translates a belief into an action. The channel here is different. The receiver's decision rule is held fixed, but surveillance changes whether information becomes public and action-guiding in a coordination game.

On the methodological side, \citet{horton2023} proposed LLMs as ``homo silicus''; \citet{aher2023} and \citet{argyle2023} use language models to replicate human-subject results and simulate survey samples, and \citet{mei2024} find chatbot behavior within the human distribution in canonical games. Subsequent work found LLMs struggle in coordination games \citep{akata2025, sun2025survey}, with model scale not predicting strategic performance \citep{jia2025}. \citet{ouyang2024} documents that alignment shifts risk preferences, while \citet{cheung2025} documents amplified moral-decision biases. Those biases are exactly why the language environment deserves direct study: what matters is how agents map politically loaded prose into action, not whether they optimize from an explicit payoff table. I therefore use narrative stakes as the implemented decision environment and audit that choice with payoff-grid checks, narrative-stakes variants, and text-only classifier diagnostics. Critical reviews by \citet{larooij2025b, larooij2026} identify validation challenges that I respond to through cross-generator robustness (Appendix~\ref{sec:cross_generator}), scramble/flip falsification, held-out classifier diagnostics, and cross-model prompt-isolation replication.

The remainder proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the theoretical framework. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the experimental design. Section~\ref{sec:results} establishes a threshold-like behavioral benchmark and shows that pre-play communication creates an informative channel. Section~\ref{sec:surveillance} tests whether an authoritarian regime can exploit that channel through surveillance. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


%% 2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
\section{The Global Game of Regime Change} \label{sec:model}

A continuum of citizens indexed by $i \in [0,1]$ simultaneously choose whether to join an uprising ($a_i = 1$) or stay home ($a_i = 0$). The regime has strength $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, drawn from a diffuse (improper uniform) prior. States $\theta < 0$ represent regimes so weak they fall without opposition under the strict rule below; states $\theta \geq 1$ represent regimes that survive even unanimous attack. The regime falls if the mass of citizens who join exceeds $\theta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:regime_falls}):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Regime falls} \iff A \equiv \int_0^1 a_i \, di > \theta.
    \label{eq:regime_falls}
\end{equation}

Payoffs depend on the citizen's action and the outcome (Eq.~\ref{eq:payoffs}):
\begin{equation}
    u_i(a_i, A, \theta) = \begin{cases}
        B & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A > \theta \\
        -C & \text{if } a_i = 1 \text{ and } A \leq \theta \\
        0 & \text{if } a_i = 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:payoffs}
\end{equation}
where $B > 0$ is the payoff to joining a successful uprising and $C > 0$ is the cost of joining a failed attempt. Non-participants receive zero regardless of the outcome.

Each citizen observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independently across citizens.

\begin{proposition}[Morris and Shin, 2003]\label{prop:ms}
In the limit of diffuse priors, there exists a unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE) in threshold strategies. An agent joins if and only if $x_i < x^*$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:signal_cutoff}), where
\begin{equation}
    x^* = \theta^* + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)
    \label{eq:signal_cutoff}
\end{equation}
$\theta^* = B/(B+C)$, and $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF.
\end{proposition}

The \textit{attack mass} $A(\theta)$, the theoretical fraction of the population that joins at regime strength $\theta$, is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A(\theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x^* - \theta}{\sigma}\right).
    \label{eq:attack_mass}
\end{equation}

This is a decreasing function of $\theta$: weaker regimes face larger uprisings.

I define $J(\theta)$ as the \textit{empirical join fraction}: the finite-sample, observed counterpart to the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$. I use $n = 25$ agents and a researcher-side state-generating distribution for $\theta$; agents are not shown the distribution or $(B, C, \sigma)$. I use the continuum $A(\theta)$ as an external behavioral benchmark throughout, not as the exact equilibrium of the implemented finite-$n$ game. Throughout the paper, $r(J, A(\theta))$ denotes the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction $J$ and the benchmark attack mass $A(\theta)$ across period-level observations.

The textbook model treats the information structure as exogenous: signals arrive, agents update, and actions follow. The experiment adds a pre-play language technology. Agents first map private briefings into messages, then other agents map their own briefing plus received messages into actions. Let $\mathcal{B}_i$ denote the sender's state-dependent private briefing object: the prose briefing generated from the normalized signal and the briefing generator's language context. The full LLM context also includes game instructions; the formal object $\mathcal{B}_i$ isolates the part held fixed across baseline and surveillance. Let $\mu_0(m\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$ denote the baseline distribution of messages $m$ generated from that briefing and $\mu_S(m\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$ the distribution generated under monitoring. The surveillance treatment changes $\mu(\cdot\mid \mathcal{B}_i)$, not $\theta$, not the private briefing, and not the receiver's decision prompt. The question is therefore whether replacing $\mu_0$ with $\mu_S$ changes $J(\theta)$.

This replacement can matter even if messages still contain first-order information about $\theta$. In a global game, a message carries two kinds of content: evidence about the fundamental and evidence about the publicness of that evidence. Direct terms, named targets, and open statements of weakness make a private signal more mutually legible; they help a receiver infer not only that the regime is weak, but that others are willing to treat the same fact as a reason to act. Monitoring can therefore preserve bad news while degrading this higher-order content. Coded language does not cause the effect mechanically; it is the evidence that the messages drawn from $\mu_S$ are less public and less action-cueing than those drawn from $\mu_0$.

The briefing generator, the system that converts $z$-scores into natural-language intelligence briefings, uses three latent sliders (direction, clarity, and coordination) to select phrases across eight evidence domains. This is less an isomorphic prose transcription of the scalar Morris--Shin private signal than a controlled language environment inspired by the canonical model, with explicit state, clarity, and actionability content. Full generator details are in Appendix~\ref{sec:briefing_examples}.

%% 3. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
\section{Experimental Design} \label{sec:design}

The experiment has two phases. First, I establish that LLM agents display threshold-like policies in the global game when private signals are conveyed in natural language: a pure treatment (private signals only), a communication treatment (pre-play messaging), and falsification tests. Second, I test whether an authoritarian regime can exploit the communication channel through surveillance. All LLM interactions use the same prompt structure across models.

In the theory (Section~\ref{sec:model}), $\theta$ is an unbounded fundamental. In the benchmark experiments, $\theta$ is drawn from a normal distribution and agents are not shown payoff parameters $(B, C)$. I compute the benchmark $A(\theta)$ under the canonical normalization $B=C=1$ ($\theta^* = 0.50$, $\sigma = 0.3$).

Throughout, $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$ is the theoretical fundamental cutoff and $x^* = \theta^* + \sigma\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$ is the signal cutoff. The empirical cutoff $\hat{\theta}^*$ is the 50\% join point of the fitted state curve $P(\text{join} \mid \theta) = 1/(1 + e^{b_0 + b_1\theta})$, where $b_1 > 0$ corresponds to a join curve that is \textit{decreasing} in $\theta$ (the expected direction), so $\hat{\theta}^* = -b_0/b_1$. In the canonical $B=C=1$ benchmark, this empirical midpoint is directly comparable to the theoretical 50\% point of $A(\theta)$; in payoff and signal-grid variants I report it as a fitted curve summary, not as the continuum equilibrium cutoff itself. I write $\kappa \equiv b_1$ for the logistic steepness parameter in tables, reserving $\beta$ for regression coefficients. Note the sign conventions: positive $\kappa$ corresponds to a join curve \textit{decreasing} in $\theta$, whereas in the agent-level logit regressions (Table~\ref{tab:regressions}) the same comparative static appears as a negative coefficient on $\theta$.

For each country--period, a base country mean and period perturbation define $z_{ct}$, and nature draws $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{ct}, 1)$. Each agent $i$ receives a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$. The platform normalizes that signal to $z_i = (x_i - z_{ct})/\sigma$ and translates $z_i$ into a multi-paragraph intelligence briefing via a deterministic generator that maps signal strength to narrative content about regime stability, economic conditions, public sentiment, and coordination prospects. This normalization is in units of private-signal noise around the country--period mean; it is not standardized by the prior-predictive signal standard deviation $\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}$. Across state and noise draws, $(x_i-z_{ct})/\sigma$ has variance $(1+\sigma^2)/\sigma^2$, so the briefing's neutral point is $x_i=z_{ct}$ rather than an absolute value of $\theta$. The benchmark $A(\theta)$ is still evaluated at each realized period-level $\theta$; the comparison is external in the sense that it does not use the $z_{ct}$-centered briefing normalization or the realized private signal draws. Agents observe the briefing, not the normalization inputs $(z_{ct}, \sigma)$ themselves. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/diagram_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Signal-to-text-to-decision pipeline. Regime strength $\theta$ generates private signals $x_i$, converted to $z$-scores and rendered into natural-language briefings via eight evidence domains and three latent sliders. Conditional on the signal draw, the briefing layer is deterministic; remaining stochasticity enters through task draws and LLM decoding.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

Agents observe only their private briefing and never the payoff parameters $(B, C)$, the noise level $\sigma$, or the prior distribution. The diffuse-prior equilibrium (Proposition~\ref{prop:ms}) serves as an external benchmark for behavioral shape and comparative statics, not as a claim about the exact equilibrium of the implemented game.

The behavioral benchmark has four core treatments: a \textit{pure global game} (independent decisions), \textit{communication} (pre-play messaging on a Watts--Strogatz network, $k=4$, $p=0.3$), \textit{scramble} (cross-period briefing permutation breaking the link between $\theta$ and the briefing received in that period), and \textit{flip} (negated $z$-scores). A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces LLM messages with generic text, and a no-message control removes peer messages entirely; together they benchmark how much the decision stage depends on message content. Additional robustness tests are in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\input{tables/tab_models.tex}

I test \PartOneNModels\ models spanning \PartOneNFamilies\ families (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The main benchmark and treatment runs use $n = 25$ agents per period-level task row and $\sigma = 0.3$; Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness} varies the agent count. Benchmark statistics are reported against the canonical $B=C=1$ attack-mass curve ($\theta^* = 0.50$); agents are not shown the payoff parameters. Main runs use temperature $= 0.7$ with a single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter and cached by request hash (Appendix~\ref{sec:decoding}); Appendix~\ref{sec:temp_robustness} varies temperature directly. Within each matched comparison, the signal-to-briefing map is held fixed and the treatment changes only the communication environment. I report the five hypotheses examined in this paper as a single hypothesis family; the four primary results in the main text---threshold alignment, signal-direction flip, communication informativeness, and surveillance---all survive Bonferroni correction over the family at $\alpha = 0.01$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses}). Throughout, $n$ denotes agents per period-level task row and $N$ denotes the number of such rows.

The unit of comparison is the matched country--period cell ($\theta$ draw plus agent-level decoding stochasticity). For agent-level regressions, standard errors are clustered at the model--country--period level. For period-level correlations, I report Fisher-transformed $z$ confidence intervals. Because periods nest within \NCountryClusters\ countries with persistent country means, period-level cells within a country are correlated; for the pooled benchmark OLS slope, clustering at the country level raises the standard error from \ClusteredSEHCOneSlope\ (HC1) to \ClusteredSECountrySlope, leaving the benchmark relationship highly significant. Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation} reports the corresponding country-clustered check for the primary surveillance contrast.

The clean surveillance treatment appends a monitoring warning to the baseline communication prompt; the decision prompt is unchanged (full prompts in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompt_example}). Thus the treatment is not a direct receiver warning but a manipulation of the message-generation environment whose downstream effect is measured on otherwise identical decision calls.

Two scope caveats apply to everything that follows. First, training-data familiarity: the Morris--Shin game and human accounts of coded speech under surveillance (samizdat, Aesopian language) are in every model's training corpus, so both threshold behavior and the direct-to-coded message shift may partly reflect reproduction of learned patterns rather than independently derived strategy. A direct audit (Appendix~\ref{sec:identification_appendix}) confirms that models can recognize the setting and articulate the experimenter's hypothesis when asked. For the measurement claim this is not disqualifying---the agents need only map language to action coherently---but it cautions against reading the behavior as agent intent, and I flag it wherever interpretation leans that way. Second, the results characterize the specific hosted model snapshots and decoding configuration listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}, not LLMs as a stable population; temperature robustness (Appendix~\ref{sec:temp_robustness}) covers the benchmark phase only, so the surveillance contrast carries no decoding-robustness check.

\section{Behavioral Benchmark} \label{sec:results}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig01_sigmoid.pdf}

\begin{result}[Monotone Threshold-Like Signal Use]
Across \PartOneNModels\ models and \PooledOLSNObs\ period-level observations in the pure global game treatment, the Pearson correlation between the empirical join fraction and the theoretical attack mass $A(\theta)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:attack_mass}) averages $r = \MeanModelRPureAttack$ ($p \PureAllModelsAttackPText$ for every model). Because $A(\theta)$ is monotone-decreasing in $\theta$, this correlation establishes monotone signal use with threshold-like shape, not equilibrium recovery; the empirical sigmoid is shifted leftward of the diffuse-prior cutoff (Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid}).\footnote{The primary model (Mistral) was run in multiple batches with \texttt{--append}, producing \PrimaryPureNObs\ rows from \DedupNUnique\ unique (country, period, $\theta$) configurations. Averaging within each configuration (deduplication) changes $r$ from \DedupRPre\ to \DedupRPost; all qualitative results are unaffected.}
\end{result}

Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid} shows the empirical sigmoid for the primary model. Correlations span $r \in [\MinModelPureRAttack, \MaxModelPureRAttack]$ across architectures (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}); the pooled OLS is $J = \PooledOLSInterceptDisp + \PooledOLSSlopeDisp\,A(\theta)$ ($R^2 = \PooledOLSRSqDisp$). \begin{result}[Communication Creates an Informative Channel]
Pre-play communication preserves the threshold structure and transmits regime-strength information through messages. A matched-cell comparison across $N = \CommNPairs$ unique (model, country, period, $\theta$) task cells yields $\Delta = \CommDeltaPPPaired$~pp ($t = \CommTStatPaired$, $p \CommPValuePairedText$).\footnote{``Matched-cell'' and ``paired'' refer to the same estimator throughout: treatment arms are compared on cells with identical task draws. The exact matching key also includes the signal draw and payoff context; see the notes to Table~\ref{tab:comm_estimators}.} Equal-weighted model averages are close to zero under both summaries ($\CommDeltaPPModelAvg$~pp unpaired; $\CommDeltaPPModelAvgPaired$~pp paired), reflecting heterogeneous model-specific effects; point estimates are negative in three of seven models.
\end{result}

The result is about the pooled matched-cell task average, not a claim that every model family has the same positive communication premium; on the equal-weighted model average, the communication effect is approximately zero. Communication preserves the signal structure ($r = \MeanModelRCommAttack$ vs.\ $\MeanModelRPureAttack$ under pure; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}; Figure~\ref{fig:communication} plots the effect by regime strength). Simple text features in baseline messages predict $\theta$, so peer messages carry real, if noisy, information. The mean communication effect is modest because messages can transmit both encouragement and caution depending on the signal realization; the equal-weighted model average is close to zero. That modest average effect is not the surveillance estimand. The surveillance question is whether changing the sender-side rule changes the downstream message environment enough to move actions.

A \textit{degraded-messages} control replaces all LLM-generated messages with generic uninformative text while preserving the two-stage protocol and omitting any surveillance framing. Against its own live-messages baseline ($N = \DegradedBaselineN$, mean join \DegradedBaselineMeanJoinPct), degraded messages drop the mean join rate to \DegradedMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \DegradedDeltaPP$~pp, $p \DegradedPValueText$), well below even the pure game (\DegradedPureMeanJoinPct). This is consistent with communication quality mattering, but the generic text may itself convey caution. A no-message control therefore provides a cleaner zero-peer-message benchmark against the main communication baseline, with join rates of \NoMsgMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta = \NoMsgDeltaPP$~pp; $N = \NoMsgN$).


%% 5. SURVEILLANCE
\section{Surveillance} \label{sec:surveillance}

In the clean surveillance treatment (equivalently, the ``clean prompt-isolation rerun''), the communication prompt is the baseline trusted-contact prompt plus one appended warning that communications are being monitored by regime security services. The manipulation affects only the communication phase; the decision prompt is unchanged. The isolation is architectural: each LLM call is stateless. The decision-stage API call receives only the briefing and the messages, with no memory of the communication-stage surveillance warning. The treatment therefore asks whether changing the \textit{sender-side} message rule is enough to change receiver actions.

Placebo and anonymous monitoring warnings sit near zero.}\label{fig:surveillance}
\end{figure}

\begin{result}[Surveillance Reduces Participation]
The clean prompt-isolation rerun reduces mean join rates in the primary model (Mistral Small Creative) from \PromptIsoMistralBaselineMeanJoinPct\ to \PromptIsoMistralMeanJoinPct, a shift of $\PromptIsoMistralDeltaPP$~pp ($p \PromptIsoMistralPValueText$; $N = \PromptIsoMistralN$). The action--attack-mass correlation is preserved ($r = \PromptIsoMistralRAttack$ vs.\ $r = \MistralCommRAttack$ under the communication baseline), indicating that surveillance operates as a level shift rather than disrupting signal processing.
\end{result}

Surveillance changes the information environment by replacing trusted-contact messages with monitored-contact messages, pushing join rates below the communication baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:surveillance}). The clean primary-model matched-cell estimate is a \PromptIsoMistralDeltaAbsPP~pp decline; pooling matched cells across all five rerun models gives $\Delta = \PromptIsoPooledMatchedDeltaPP$~pp ($N = \PromptIsoPooledMatchedN$, $p \PromptIsoPooledMatchedPText$). Placebo variants using the same baseline-plus-warning template---monitored for research (no consequences) and aggregated anonymously---produce no significant deviation from the communication baseline in the primary model (\PlaceboSurvDeltaPP~pp and \AnonSurvDeltaPP~pp, both $p > 0.9$; Appendix~\ref{sec:surv_isolation}); Llama 3.3 70B placebo variants are small but imprecisely estimated. Across the clean prompt-isolation rerun set, \PromptIsoNegativeModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ models move in the negative direction on matched-cell support (equal-weighted mean $\Delta = \PromptIsoMeanDeltaPP$~pp; \PromptIsoSigFivePctModels/\PromptIsoNModels\ individually significant at 5\%; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prompt_isolation}). I treat the non-primary evidence as limited sign replication. Several rows have only 20 matched cells because the shorter baseline communication grids and later 1,000-cell surveillance grids were not nested. The effect is smaller in Qwen3 235B ($\Delta = \PromptIsoQwenLDeltaPP$~pp, $p = \PromptIsoQwenLPValue$ on 20 matched cells) than in the other models, and is not individually significant under the paired test, but the sign is the same. The strongest evidence is the primary-model matched result; the cross-model rows show directionally consistent but imprecise replication.

The interpretation requires separating four possible channels. The warning could deter receivers directly, but the stateless decision prompt rules this out in the main treatment; the hold-fixed test below measures direct deterrence only when the warning is deliberately moved to the decision stage. Monitoring could instead remove information about regime weakness, which the content evidence tests by asking whether bad news disappears or changes form. It could degrade messages for downstream judgments generally, and the private-bet discriminant test shows movement on a non-action decision label, although a bet on regime fall can still depend on beliefs about others' participation and so does not isolate a structurally non-coordination component. Or it could reduce the public, action-cueing value of messages---the higher-order channel targeted by the belief-elicitation contrast, the no-message controls, the coded-language shift, and the cross-model writer--reader rotation. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:identification_map} summarizes the full map.

\paragraph{Locating the channel: communication poisoning.}

By ``communication poisoning'' I mean something specific: monitored messages become less useful inputs for the receiver's coordination decision. In global-game terms, the treatment can leave information about the fundamental $\theta$ in the message while changing what the message says about strategic complementarity: whether others are willing to condition on the same signal. In LLM terms, the prompt intervention changes the conditional distribution of generated peer messages, while the receiver prompt and private briefing are held fixed. Three distinct message properties could change: (a) factual information about regime strength $\theta$, (b) coordination cues about what other citizens are willing to say or do, and (c) risk/fear salience transmitted by tone. The evidence is not that surveillance simply deletes bad news about the regime. Monitoring changes the language of peer messages, and the changed messages are less action-guiding for receivers; the dictionary and classifier evidence below identifies \emph{what} changed in the text, not which textual property drives the action response. I read the text evidence as most consistent with a loss of mutual legibility, but the load-bearing empirical claim is more general: surveillance degrades message usefulness under a fixed receiver decision prompt. Surveillance therefore acts on the form in which factual content circulates, not on information volume; whether the operative property is reduced publicness or heightened fear salience is not separately identified in this design (Table~\ref{tab:identification_map}).

Message content changes systematically, and in one direction. A logistic classifier on uni- and bi-grams distinguishes baseline from surveillance messages with \MsgClassifierAcc\% accuracy on a held-out sample of $N = \MsgClassifierNTest$ messages (chance = 50\%; primary model). The same finding replicates on class-balanced Llama 3.3 70B and Qwen3 30B samples (\MsgClassifierLlamaAcc\% and \MsgClassifierQwenSAcc\% accuracy, respectively); in \MsgDirectCodedRepModels/\MsgDirectCodedNModels\ measured models, direct references fall and coded-metaphor references rise. The shift is not toward fewer explicit weakness references: in the primary model, references to regime weakness \emph{rise} from \MsgWeaknessBase\% to \MsgWeaknessSurv\% of messages ($z = \MsgWeaknessZ$), while strength references drop from \MsgStrengthBase\% to \MsgStrengthSurv\%. Rather, the shift is from direct to coded language: direct references to the regime, security forces, and streets fall from \MsgDirectRegimeBase\% to \MsgDirectRegimeSurv\% of messages ($\Delta = \MsgDirectRegimeDeltaPP$~pp), replaced by metaphorical phrasings (``walls are cracking,'' ``ground shifting,'' ``heads down,'' ``feels different''; \MsgCodedMetaphorBase\% to \MsgCodedMetaphorSurv\%, $\Delta = \MsgCodedMetaphorDeltaPP$~pp). The n-gram classifier in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:msg_features} shows separability of the peer-message distributions; the direct-reference and coded-metaphor rates are separate dictionary counts. I do not interpret codedness as a separately randomized mechanism; it is the measured textual signature of the treated message distribution, where weakness cues remain present but arrive in a less direct, less mutually legible form. With the decision prompt unchanged, the behavioral effect is localized to this sender-generated message distribution. A further interpretive caveat applies on the sender side: the response to the monitoring warning may partly reflect alignment training---caution about producing subversive content when monitoring is salient---rather than the strategic self-censorship of a simulated citizen. Trinity Large's elevated provider content-filter failures (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}) are direct evidence that safety layers activate on this material. The design identifies the downstream effect of the changed messages on receivers, not the sender's motive for changing them.

A dictionary audit of receivers' free-text decision rationales sharpens the receiver-side picture. Explicit references to monitoring or surveillance are rare in both arms (\RatSurvInferBasePct\ of \RatNBase\ valid baseline rationales vs.\ \RatSurvInferSurvPct\ of \RatNSurv\ under surveillance), so the action response does not run through receivers verbalizing an inference that they are watched. Nor do rationales become more fearful: fear and caution references \emph{fall} from \RatFearBasePct\ to \RatFearSurvPct\ ($p \RatFearPText$), weighing against fear contagion through message tone as the dominant receiver-side channel. What changes is engagement with the peer channel itself: references to contacts and peer messages drop from \RatPeerBasePct\ to \RatPeerSurvPct\ ($p \RatPeerPText$), while regime-weakness references rise from \RatWeakBasePct\ to \RatWeakSurvPct. Receivers still read bad news but lean less on the messages when those messages arrive coded---the pattern the actionability interpretation predicts. These are coarse, post hoc dictionary counts on stated rationales, not elicited mechanisms, and stated rationales need not reflect the model's actual computation.

The degraded-messages control (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Communication) exogenously replaces all messages with generic text, without any surveillance framing, and drops joining by \DegradedDeltaAbsPP~pp (from its \DegradedBaselineMeanJoinPct\ live-messages baseline to \DegradedMeanJoinPct). That comparison uses its own live-message rerun. Two implications follow. First, decision-stage agents use message content. Second, and importantly for interpretation, \emph{any} degradation of message quality depresses participation in this environment, and the degraded-messages drop exceeds the surveillance effect itself; the design does not include a treatment that induces coded or vague style without monitoring framing, so the evidence cannot separate surveillance-specific loss of publicness from a generic loss of message informativeness combined with cautionary tone. The no-message control uses the main communication baseline and gives a cleaner zero-peer-message benchmark (primary model: \NoMsgMeanJoinPct; $\Delta = \NoMsgDeltaPP$~pp, $p = \NoMsgPValue$). Across the three models with no-message data (Mistral, Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen3 30B), removing peer messages reduces joining in all three cases ($-$\NoMsgDeltaAbsMinPP\ to $-$\NoMsgDeltaAbsMaxPP~pp), though the primary-model estimate is the smallest and only marginally significant; this is consistent with decision-stage agents weighting peer messages as informational input. Where surveillance lies relative to this floor varies. In Mistral it falls below silence; in Llama and Qwen3 30B it remains above silence but below baseline communication. Across models, then, monitored messages are not always worse than silence, but monitoring degraded the communication channel in all three models examined; the below-silence pattern, which suggests active poisoning beyond information removal, appears only in the primary model.

Elicited punishment risk is ${\approx}\,\PunishRiskMean/10$ across all conditions with negligible differences between JOIN and STAY agents (Table~\ref{tab:punishment_risk}). Because the elicitation sits near the top of the scale in every condition, the instrument may be ceiling-limited; it does not separately identify fear salience in message tone, but it weighs against a simple receiver-side story in which surveilled messages mainly work by making agents report higher punishment risk.

\paragraph{Belief Elicitations and Actions.}
I elicit second-order beliefs (SOB) with the question, ``What percentage of citizens will choose to JOIN?'' The \emph{primary belief sample} is the post-decision elicitation in which the agent does not re-see the peer messages on the belief prompt; this keeps the instrument from mechanically re-exposing the treated text after the action. In that sample, communication agents report \SOBCommMeanPct\ and surveillance agents report \SOBSurvMeanPct\ ($\Delta=\SOBDeltaPP$~pp, $p=\SOBPValue$), while joining falls from \BeliefCommMeanJoinPct\ to \BeliefSurvMeanJoinPct\ ($\Delta=\BeliefCommSurvActionDeltaPP$~pp). Stability here is a bounded contrast, not only a failure to reject: the agent-level 95\% confidence interval on the SOB shift spans [$\SOBDeltaCILoPP$, $\SOBDeltaCIHiPP$]~pp, an order of magnitude smaller than the action shift, though within-period correlation of agent reports would widen this interval. First-order success beliefs are also close (\BeliefCommMeanBeliefPct\ vs.\ \BeliefSurvMeanBeliefPct). A point report of expected participation is not the whole global-game object. The action value depends on whether participation clears the regime threshold and on confidence that other receivers parse the same messages as action-relevant. Those objects can move even when a coarse report of expected joining does not. This is the coordination-game version of a beliefs-to-actions gap: factual content and action value are not the same object. The belief/action contrast is therefore a measured fact, not a causal mediation claim. The same agents who report nearly unchanged expectations about others' participation choose substantially less participation themselves.

The belief evidence is sensitive to the elicitation information set. When the belief prompt includes the received peer messages, reported beliefs and SOB move sharply under surveillance (Appendix~\ref{sec:belief_appendix}, Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial}); a parallel pre-decision elicitation in the same messages-included condition produces $\Delta\,\text{SOB} = \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgPre$~pp ($p < \BFSurvDeltaSOBMsgPreP$), close to the post-decision contrast ($\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pp). The pre-decision $\times$ messages-excluded cell could not be collected before the primary model was retired from the API provider (Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation}). That missing cell would help distinguish timing and re-exposure effects in the restricted information set, but simple post-decision action rationalization by itself would tend to push expected participation downward when surveillance lowers joining; it does not explain a near-zero messages-excluded contrast on its own. The available cells show that re-exposing peer messages matters; they do not rule out timing effects for the primary messages-excluded contrast.

A separate hold-fixed test replays baseline messages verbatim while adding a surveillance warning to the \textit{decision} prompt, producing a $\FixedMsgDeltaPP$~pp drop ($p \FixedMsgPValueText$). This shows a direct decision-stage channel exists, but it is absent in the main design, where the decision prompt contains no surveillance reference. The main treatment isolates the indirect channel: communication poisoning.

\paragraph{Task contrast: private-bet versus coordination decisions.}
A complementary discriminant test holds the surveillance manipulation fixed but swaps the task. In Llama 3.3 70B, I keep the briefing pipeline and messaging stage identical, including the surveillance prompt, but reframe the decision-stage prompt as a private bet on regime fall (payoff independent of the respondent's own action). Surveillance still moves the bet by $\CrossTaskDeltaPP$~pp ($p = \CrossTaskPValue$; $N = \CrossTaskN$ per condition; baseline \CrossTaskBaselineMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ surveillance \CrossTaskSurvMeanJoinPct), so the treated messages affect downstream judgment even when the respondent is not choosing the strategic action. This is not a clean non-coordination placebo: a bet on regime fall can still depend on beliefs about whether others will participate. The full Llama prompt-isolation coordination-task estimate is larger ($-\CrossTaskCoordTaskDeltaAbsPP$~pp), but the private-bet and coordination samples are not matched on identical task cells, and the later rotation-matched within-Llama subset is smaller and closer in magnitude to the private-bet effect. I therefore treat the comparison as a descriptive task contrast, not as evidence that the coordination-task effect is generally larger or as a point-identified decomposition.

\paragraph{Cross-model writer--reader rotation.}
A standing concern with the within-model design is reflexivity: writer model and reader model are the same, so the reader could be responding to stylistic artifacts of its own family rather than to a content property of surveilled messages. I therefore test bidirectional rotation on matched (country, period, $\theta$) cells. \emph{Llama writes, Qwen3 30B reads}: surveillance reduces Qwen joining by $\XModelLlamaQwenDeltaPP$~pp ($p \XModelLlamaQwenPValueText$, $N = \XModelLlamaQwenNPairs$), comparable to the within-Llama matched effect ($\XModelWithinLlamaDeltaPP$~pp on identical cells). \emph{Qwen3 30B writes, Llama reads}: the reverse direction gives a smaller but still significant cross-model effect ($\XModelQwenLlamaDeltaPP$~pp, $p \XModelQwenLlamaPValueText$, $N = \XModelQwenLlamaNPairs$). Both directions rule out the reader merely recognizing its own family's style: at least part of the effect transmits across model families through message content alone. The cross-direction asymmetry is unexplained, and its magnitude cautions against treating the full within-model effect as content-driven.


%% 6. CONCLUSION
\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

The headline result is a sender-side surveillance effect in a controlled global game: monitoring can suppress coordinated action without ever appearing in the receiver's decision problem. LLM citizens first reproduce the canonical comparative static, with participation falling as regime strength rises. Communication then turns private prose signals into social information. When monitoring enters only during that messaging stage, point estimates for joining are negative in all five clean prompt-isolation reruns (four individually significant, several on small matched-cell support) even though, in the primary belief sample (post-decision, peer messages not re-shown to the agent), reported expectations about how many others will join remain approximately stable. Placebo warnings do not replicate the effect in the primary model, and message controls show that downstream agents use peer messages as part of their decision environment. The same channel that transmits coordination-relevant information is also the channel an authoritarian regime can degrade, by damaging the conversion of information into a shared reason to act rather than by removing the information itself.

The mechanism evidence extends beyond the main treatment contrast. Surveillance moves the private-bet task as well, so the treated messages affect downstream judgment outside the main JOIN/STAY action prompt; the task is not a clean non-coordination placebo because beliefs about regime fall may still depend on expectations about participation. In the full Llama prompt-isolation comparison the coordination-task effect is larger than the private-bet effect, but that contrast is descriptive rather than a matched residual, and the smaller rotation-matched subset is closer in magnitude to the private-bet estimate. Message text also shifts from direct statements toward metaphorical and deniable phrasing, consistent with a loss of coordination-cueing value beyond a generic judgment shift, though the design does not separately identify which textual property carries the action response. In language-mediated global games, surveillance can degrade the higher-order content of information without fully degrading its first-order content. It can leave bad news in circulation while making that news less mutually legible as something others will act on. More broadly, the experiment isolates a channel by which repression could target the publicness and actionability of information, not just its factual content. Whether this characterizes how authoritarian regimes \emph{actually} deploy monitoring is a separate empirical question; the contribution here is to isolate the communication-poisoning channel under controlled prompts and matched decision contexts.

Several limitations remain. Whether the underlying mechanism is approximate Bayesian reasoning or a learned heuristic that matches BNE is still open, and training-data familiarity with both the game and the sociology of coded speech (Section~\ref{sec:design}) means neither behavior need be independently derived strategy. The sender-side response may partly reflect alignment training rather than simulated self-censorship (Section~\ref{sec:surveillance}). The surveillance warning uses a single phrasing; paraphrase variants analogous to the stakes-ladder v2 conditions were not run, so sensitivity to the warning's wording and imperative framing is untested. The experiments are static, while real coordination problems are dynamic \citep{angeletos2007a, chassang2010}; in repeated interaction, receivers may learn to decode euphemism, so the static effect is plausibly an upper bound. The results characterize specific hosted model snapshots under one decoding configuration, and the primary model's endpoint is retired: the headline arm can be re-analyzed from the archived request cache but not re-queried. The deepest limitation is external validity: what LLM agents do in a coordination game is evidence about how language-trained agents respond to this information structure, not necessarily about how humans would play it. Cross-model benchmark and clean-surveillance replication suggest the qualitative pattern is not specific to one model family, though the non-primary surveillance replications are imprecise. The experimental template is reusable, and \ifanonymous the data and code are provided in the de-identified replication archive.\else the data and code are publicly available.\fi

\section{Complete Prompt Example} \label{sec:prompt_example}

The following shows the complete text that an LLM receives for a single decision. The \textit{decision prompt} is:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. You only have your private briefing below. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent This is followed by the agent's intelligence briefing (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_briefings} for complete examples at $z \in \{-2, 0, +2\}$). In the \textit{communication} treatment, agents first receive:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen in a country with an authoritarian regime. You have received a private intelligence briefing. You are now in contact with a small number of trusted people. Based on your briefing, write a brief message (1--2 sentences) to share your read of the situation. Be natural---you're talking to people you trust.''
\end{quote}

\noindent After exchanging messages on the network, agents receive a separate communication-decision prompt:

\begin{quote}
\small
``You are a citizen deciding whether to JOIN an uprising or STAY home. Use your private briefing and messages from trusted contacts. You do not know how many others will JOIN. Start your first line with exactly JOIN or STAY, then give 1--2 concise sentences of reasoning.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The user message then contains the agent's briefing under ``YOUR INTELLIGENCE BRIEFING'' and the neighbor messages under ``MESSAGES FROM TRUSTED CONTACTS.'' The pure-only sentence ``You only have your private briefing below'' does not appear in communication decision calls. In the \textit{surveillance} variant, the communication prompt appends:

\begin{quote}
\small
``IMPORTANT: You have reason to believe that your communications are being monitored by regime security services, and messages deemed subversive could have serious consequences for you and your contacts.''
\end{quote}

\noindent The decision prompt is \textit{unchanged} under surveillance. It contains no mention of monitoring. Any behavioral effect therefore operates through the content of the (now self-censored) messages.


\section{Belief Elicitation} \label{sec:belief_appendix}

Beliefs are elicited on a 0--100 probability scale immediately after each decision. The first-order question is ``How likely is the uprising to succeed?'' The second-order question is ``What percentage of citizens will choose to JOIN?'' The primary belief elicitation prompt includes the private briefing and the agent's own decision, but not the received peer messages; this keeps the elicitation from mechanically re-exposing the treatment text after the action. The main-text values (\SOBCommMeanPct\ vs.\ \SOBSurvMeanPct; join \BeliefCommMeanJoinPct\ vs.\ \BeliefSurvMeanJoinPct) come from this original primary belief run and Figure~\ref{fig:second_order_beliefs}. A factorial variant tests information-set sensitivity. Table~\ref{tab:belief_factorial} crosses condition (communication vs.\ surveillance) with elicitation information set (messages included vs.\ excluded). Without messages, first-order beliefs barely shift under surveillance ($\BFSurvDeltaBelNoMsg$~pp, $p = \BFSurvDeltaBelNoMsgP$) and second-order beliefs are effectively unchanged ($\BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsg$~pp, $p = \BFSurvDeltaSOBNoMsgP$), while actions shift $\BFActionDeltaPP$~pp. With messages included, beliefs shift significantly ($\BFSurvDeltaBelMsg$~pp and $\BFSurvDeltaSOBMsg$~pp, both $p < \BFSurvDeltaBelMsgP$). The belief/action contrast is therefore an instrument-sensitive descriptive fact, not a claim that surveillance leaves all beliefs unchanged under every elicitation prompt. \paragraph{Contamination and demand audit.}
Following \citet{horton2023}, I probe directly whether models recognize the experiment. For \HortonNModels\ accessible paper models, \HortonNSamples\ fresh post hoc calls per model per question present the decision prompt and a $z=0$ briefing without asking the model to play. Asked whether the setup resembles a formal model, \HortonGameRecogPct\ of responses name the global game or Morris--Shin specifically, while \HortonCoordRecogPct\ identify a coordination or collective-action setting generically. Asked to predict the effect of the surveillance manipulation when it is described explicitly, \HortonPredictLowerPct\ of responses predict lower participation. Hypothesis recognition is therefore available to the models when prompted, and the design does not rely on participants being unaware. The receiver-side estimate is instead identified by the architectural exclusion of the warning from the decision prompt, and the sender-side placebo variants show the response tracks stated consequences rather than the mere mention of monitoring. The audit cannot be run on the retired primary model.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:classifiers} reports an action-prediction diagnostic, not a baseline-versus-surveillance condition classifier. Classifiers trained on pure-treatment briefing-body text predict how agents would act from the private briefing alone; when applied to surveillance-treatment briefings, they predict join rates near the non-surveilled benchmark and miss the observed action drop. Table~\ref{tab:bc_classifier} asks a parallel question for narrative stakes headers: whether baseline briefing-body features can predict header-induced behavioral shifts. They cannot, which is expected because the headers are outside the briefing-body feature set. In contrast, a classifier on the actual peer messages, which are LLM-generated and downstream of the surveillance prompt, separates the two conditions sharply (\MsgClassifierAcc\% accuracy on $N = \MsgClassifierNTest$ held-out messages; Table~\ref{tab:msg_features}). Together the classifier exercises locate the manipulated text surface: private briefing text does not explain the action shift, stakes headers change behavior outside the baseline feature set, and surveilled peer messages change visibly.

\subsection{Finite-\texorpdfstring{$n$}{n} Aggregation Check}
The continuum benchmark $\theta^* = B/(B+C)$ assumes a measure-zero agent and diffuse priors. With $n = 25$ discrete agents, the regime falls when $\sum a_i > n\theta$ (a binomial count), so the exact equilibrium object for the implemented game differs from the continuum limit. Table~\ref{tab:finite_n} reports a narrower check: for each model, I fit a logistic to the observed join curve (70\% train), then compute $\Pr(\text{Binom}(25, \hat{p}(\theta)) > 25\theta)$ as the predicted regime fall rate. Predicted and empirical fall rates correlate at $r \geq \FiniteNMinR$ (two-decimal rounding) for every model (Mistral: $r = \FiniteNPrimaryR$).

\section{Implementation Details} \label{sec:implementation}

\label{sec:decoding}
Unless otherwise stated, LLM calls use temperature $= 0.7$, \texttt{max\_tokens} $= 512$, single sample per decision, routed through OpenRouter. Model identifiers are listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}; exact request payloads and responses are cached by hash because hosted model snapshots may change over time.\footnote{The primary model identifier \texttt{mistralai/mistral-small-creative} was retired from OpenRouter after data collection. All cached request/response pairs used in this paper are archived in the replication package, but new experiments on that exact endpoint cannot be issued; replicators wishing to extend the primary-model arm should consult the data archive rather than re-querying the live API.}

Each country has a researcher-side state mean $\bar{z}_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2)$; each period draws a perturbation $\eta_{ct} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$ and sets $z_{ct}=\bar{z}_c+\eta_{ct}$. Regime strength is then drawn as $\theta_{ct} \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{ct}, 1)$, and private signals are $x_i = \theta_{ct} + \varepsilon_i$ with $\sigma = 0.3$. These distributions generate experiment cells; they are not shown to agents as explicit priors. The communication network is Watts--Strogatz ($k = 4$, $p = 0.3$), constructed once per run from the master seed and reused across period-level task rows; agents are reinitialized in each row. All random draws use a master seed logged in per-run JSON manifests; LLM responses are cached by request hash for exact reproducibility.

\label{sec:parse_errors}Responses are parsed for JOIN/STAY tokens. Combined error rates are below 2\% in every listed treatment for \ParseErrModelsBelowTwo\ of \ParseErrNModels\ models. The two exceptions are GPT-OSS 120B, whose unparseable-response rate reaches 3.1--3.3\% in the scramble and flip falsification arms only, and Trinity Large, which has elevated API errors (${\approx}\,\TrinityAPIErrorPct\%$) due to provider content filtering, so its missingness remains a model-specific limitation. All statistics use \texttt{join\_fraction\_valid} (Table~\ref{tab:parse_errors}).
\input{tables/tab_parse_errors.tex}

The dissent floor parameter (default 0.25) controls the minimum probability of contrary-valence cues in any briefing. With the logistic direction slider ($\text{slope} = 0.8$), the valence-channel separation between extreme signals ($z = \pm 2$) is $0.664 \cdot (1 - 2\cdot\text{floor})$: at the default $0.25$, the valence channel retains 50\% of the floor-free maximum; halving to $0.125$ retains 75\%; at $0.50$ the valence channel collapses to zero by construction. Clarity and coordination sliders, which depend on $z$ independently of the floor, continue to carry signal at all floor values, so the threshold relation is not knife-edge in this design choice.

Table~\ref{tab:slider_values} reports the three slider values (direction, clarity, coordination) at representative $z$-scores for the briefing generator. The direction slider is logistic in $z$ (slope 0.8). Clarity is U-shaped: $1 - \exp[-(|z|/1.0)^2]$, so it is lowest near the ambiguous midpoint and approaches 1 in the extremes (maximally clear). Coordination is $\Lambda(-0.6\,z) = 1/(1 + e^{0.6z})$, decreasing in $z$: weak-regime signals ($z < 0$) imply a more open coordination climate. All three jointly determine phrase selection across eight evidence domains.
